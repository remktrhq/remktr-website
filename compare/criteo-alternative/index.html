Retailers monetising their own inventory want Kevel or Moloco. If the reason is fee opacity alone, that is usually a renegotiation, not a migration.

Most alternative lists answer the wrong question

The standard article ranks ten platforms by star rating and leaves you to guess. That order is meaningless, because the products in it are not substitutes for each other. Kevel serves retailers building their own ad platforms. Commerce Yield serves the same audience. The Trade Desk buys the open internet. Amazon DSP buys Amazon's signal. Skai and Pacvue are management layers that sit above several of these.

So start from the reason. In practice there are five, and each has a different correct answer.

  • Your demand is concentrated on Amazon and open-internet retail media is not where your buyers are.
  • You need broader inventory and channel control than a retail media network gives.
  • You are a retailer or marketplace, not a brand, and you want to monetise your own inventory.
  • Nobody on your team is operating the platform, so performance is drifting.
  • The fee stack is opaque and you suspect you are overpaying.

Only three of those are actually migrations.

Reason one: your demand lives on Amazon

This is the most common case we see, and it is a genuine mismatch rather than a vendor failure. Criteo is strong at reaching shoppers across many retailers and across the open internet. If eighty percent of your revenue closes on Amazon, the thing you actually need is a buy tied to Amazon's shopping signal, with attribution against Amazon's own purchase data.

That is Amazon DSP. You can run it three ways: Amazon's own managed service, which typically requires a minimum investment of USD 50,000 according to Amazon's product page; self-service, if you have a trader; or through an agency holding seats, which is where minimums are usually more flexible.

The reason this matters beyond reach is measurement. In Amazon Marketing Cloud you can build a holdout, compare matched groups, and see DSP and sponsored ads reconciled against each other instead of both claiming the same order. No open-internet retail media network can do that against Amazon purchases, because it does not have the data.

Reasons two and three: reach, or the other side of the market

If you need broader open-internet control, The Trade Desk is the serious independent option, with transparent programmatic buying and a much wider inventory remit than a retail media network. It is a different discipline to run and a different cost base. Adform is worth a look if you want DSP, ad server and data management in one place. Neither will give you retailer-specific shopper signal the way Criteo does — that is the trade.

If you are a retailer or marketplace rather than a brand, you are shopping in a different aisle entirely. Kevel provides ad APIs for building your own retail media platform, chosen mostly for flexibility and control over the stack. Moloco is the machine-learning-heavy option for retailers who want performance without building the auction themselves. Criteo's own Commerce Yield sits in this space too, so "leaving Criteo" here may just mean moving products inside Criteo.

Do not let a comparison table put these next to each other as if a brand and a retailer were buying the same thing.

Reason four: nobody is running it

Switching platforms because performance drifted, when the underlying cause is that the account has one part-time owner, buys you a migration and the same result six months later. It is the most expensive mistake in this category.

The test is simple. Ask who touched the account last week, what they changed, and what evidence they used. If the honest answer is that a report gets read monthly and nothing changes, the platform is not your constraint.

Reason five: the fee stack — and why that is often not a migration

Criteo does not publish rates. What it does publish, in its Commerce Max terms of service, is the structure: a Demand Side Platform Fee, plus a Managed Service Fee where managed service is used, both a percentage of working media spend, with a Retailer Audience Data Fee for off-site audiences and possible third-party technology fees on top. All of it is calculated against working media spend, which the terms define as the media cost payable to retailers and publishers for their inventory.

Read that as leverage rather than as a reason to leave. If you cannot see your effective rate, ask for it in writing, then ask what it steps down to at higher spend. At $100,000 a month in media, one percentage point is $12,000 a year — a bigger prize than most migrations deliver, and available without changing a single campaign.

Only migrate on fees when you have the number, have asked for a better one, and have been told no.

Who should stay with Criteo

Plenty of readers should, and the honest recommendation is to stay put.

If your sales are genuinely spread across many retailers, Criteo's reach across retail media and the open internet is the product doing that job, and no Amazon-focused service replaces it. If you run self-service and have a competent operator, the Managed Service Fee does not apply and your cost base is already lean. If you are a retailer monetising inventory, Commerce Yield is purpose-built and switching to a brand-side DSP makes no sense at all.

The clean case for leaving is narrow: Amazon dominates your revenue, you cannot prove display is adding incremental orders, and the measurement you need lives in Amazon's own data. Then a change of platform is the point rather than a symptom.

Which one you should actually pick

Stay with Criteo if your sales are spread across many retailers, if you run self-service well, or if you are a retailer monetising your own inventory. Move to Amazon DSP — self-serve, or managed by reMKTR — when Amazon dominates revenue and you need display proved incremental against Amazon's own purchase data rather than a platform report.

What to do with this

Before you switch, write down the one number the switch has to change. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. A tool that reports that number and a service that removes it are different purchases; pick the one you have hours for.

Common questions

What is the closest direct alternative to Criteo?

There is no single one, because Criteo sells to brands, agencies and retailers with different products. For a brand buying retail media, the nearest comparisons are Amazon DSP for Amazon-led demand and The Trade Desk for open-internet reach. For a retailer, Kevel and Moloco are the comparison set.

Is Amazon DSP better than Criteo?

Better at a different job. Amazon DSP has Amazon's shopping signal and lets you measure against Amazon purchase data in Amazon Marketing Cloud. Criteo reaches shoppers across many retailers on the open internet. If Amazon is most of your revenue, the first is the closer match.

Do I need a $50,000 minimum for Amazon DSP?

Amazon's own product page describes a typical minimum investment of USD 50,000 for its managed-service option, and notes this varies by country. Buying through an agency that holds seats is the usual route to more flexible minimums — ask any agency what theirs is, in writing.

How long does switching take?

Plan for a measurement gap, not just a technical migration. Historical data does not move between platforms, so build the new baseline deliberately — ideally with a holdout running from the first week, so the first performance question after the switch has an honest answer.

Should I run two platforms in parallel?

Sometimes, and briefly. Parallel running gives you a comparison but also double-counts conversions, since both platforms will claim the same orders. If you do it, decide the arbitration rule in advance and reconcile in one place rather than arguing about two dashboards afterwards.
