The short answer

Amazon DSP is the demand-side platform for programmatic advertising, not the Delivery Service Partner courier programme. Searching for partner reviews mostly returns directory listings and award announcements, which are credentials rather than opinions. Both are useful if you read them for what they certify and ignore what they imply.

Most of what this search returns is not a review

Type this query and you will get three kinds of page, only one of which is what you asked for.

  • Directory profiles. Amazon's partner directory lists service offerings, expertise levels and marketplaces. It is maintained by the partner and gated by Amazon. Everything on it is a claim the partner has made and Amazon has permitted.
  • Award announcements. Amazon runs a Partner Awards programme recognising campaigns and partners. An award is a judged credential with a date and a category attached.
  • Actual reviews. Rare in this corner of the market, because programmatic display is usually one component of a wider engagement and the person writing the review experienced the whole relationship rather than the media buying.

None of these is worthless. The mistake is reading a credential as if it were a satisfaction rating, or a satisfaction rating as if it were a performance result. This page is about extracting real signal from each type.

The disambiguation first: Amazon DSP is the demand-side platform, the programmatic advertising product. Amazon Delivery Service Partner is the courier franchise programme. Reviews of one tell you nothing about the other, and search results blend them freely.

How to read a partner directory profile properly

A profile is a structured claim, and structured claims can be interrogated. Four things are worth extracting from any listing before you take a call.

  • The service list, read for omissions. If DSP is not named explicitly, the firm may still run it, but it is not what they lead with. That is informative. Conversely, a firm listing thirty services is telling you something about depth.
  • Marketplaces and regions. A partner listed for markets you do not sell in is carrying overhead you will indirectly fund; a partner missing the market you are about to launch in is a future gap.
  • Expertise level and status. Amazon awards advanced and verified partner status for demonstrated expertise and advertiser growth over time. It is a gate worth applying, not a ranking worth trusting.
  • What is absent. Directory profiles do not carry fee structures, minimums, team size per account, or any statement of measurement method. Every one of those is more predictive of your experience than anything the profile does contain.

Read profiles to build a shortlist and to write better questions. Do not read them to rank.

Awards, and the difference between a real credential and a claim

Some credentials in this market are checkable and specific. Those are worth real weight, because a firm that misstates one can be caught.

Podean's 2023 Amazon Global Expansion Award is a good example of the shape: a named award, from Amazon, in a stated year, for a stated thing. You can verify it exists, and it tells you something narrow but true — that Amazon judged their international marketplace work strong at that point. It does not tell you what their DSP measurement practice looks like today, and no award does.

Compare that with the softer claims that fill agency sites: "leading", "top-rated", "trusted by hundreds of brands". Not dishonest, usually, but not checkable either, which means they cannot be used to discriminate between two candidates. The practical rule is to give weight in proportion to how easily you could catch an error. A dated, named, third-party credential ranks above a self-description every time, and both rank below a reference call.

Programme details in this area do change — tiers, criteria and benefits get revised. Check the current position on Amazon's own pages rather than in a third-party summary written a year ago, including this one.

Build your own evidence file in a week

Five steps, roughly a day each, producing something better than any published review could give you.

  • Day one — shortlist from the directory. Filter on DSP, your marketplaces and your region. Note who leads with programmatic and who leads with search.
  • Day two — read every candidate's published method. Not case studies: method. Anyone who has written publicly about how they design a test has exposed themselves to correction, which is a form of accountability.
  • Day three — check team tenure. Public professional profiles will tell you how long the senior people have been in place. Continuity predicts engagement quality better than headcount does.
  • Day four — two reference calls each, and ask for one client who left. The exit story is the most honest thing you will hear in the whole process.
  • Day five — one uniform brief, one uniform question set. Same media budget, same inclusions list, same eight questions, annual totals rather than monthly fees.

Two questions that produce more truth than any review

"Tell me about a test that came back weaker than you expected." Every partner who genuinely measures has one. The answer reveals three things at once: whether they run experiments at all, whether they tell clients about disappointing results, and what they changed afterwards. A candidate who cannot produce an example either does not test or does not share.

"How do you stop display and sponsored ads claiming the same order?" This is the technical question that separates measurement from reporting. If both channels are credited with the same purchase, the blended return looks better than reality and the gap widens as budget grows. The reconciliation happens in Amazon Marketing Cloud or it does not happen at all, and a partner who has never done it will usually say something about "holistic reporting" instead of describing a method.

Ask both of every candidate, including us. They take four minutes and they outperform an afternoon of reading star ratings.

Beware evidence that predates the product

A specific trap in this market, and it applies to software vendors and agencies alike: a great deal of the published evaluation you will find describes a version of the company that no longer exists.

On the software side, several retail media tools have repositioned substantially — around data layers, AI agents and machine-readable gateways — while the reviews ranking for their names describe the previous product. In at least one case the tool a review discusses was absorbed into a larger suite and no longer exists as a separate purchase, yet pricing figures for it still circulate freely. Any number attached to a retired product is worse than no number, because it feels specific.

On the agency side the same thing happens through acquisition. A firm bought two years ago may be excellent, unchanged, and better resourced — or it may have a different leadership team, a different methodology and a different pricing structure while the reviews still describe the founder-led shop. Nothing dishonest has occurred; the review corpus simply cannot update itself.

Three checks that cost ten minutes each:

  • Date every piece of evidence and discard anything whose subject has been acquired, rebranded or restructured since it was written, unless you can confirm continuity.
  • Compare the review's description to the current site. If the services, positioning or product names have moved, the review is describing something else.
  • Ask the firm directly what has changed in the last two years. The willingness to answer plainly is itself evidence, and it is the question most likely to surface a change that matters to you.

Applied to us as well: check what we say today against what any third party wrote about us previously, and if the two disagree, ask.

Which one you should actually pick

Directory profiles build your shortlist, badges act as a gate, and dated named awards carry narrow but real weight. None of them measure performance. Generate your own evidence instead: published method, team tenure, portfolio figures with a denominator, and reference calls including one client who left. Ask every candidate about a test that disappointed them.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Where do I find Amazon DSP partner reviews?

Mostly you will find directory listings and award announcements rather than reviews. Amazon's partner directory is the best starting point for verifiable detail; genuine written reviews of DSP work specifically are scarce because display is usually one part of a larger engagement.

Is a verified partner better than an advanced partner?

They are different recognition levels within the same programme, both earned by demonstrating Amazon Ads expertise and delivering advertiser growth over time. Criteria and benefits are revised periodically, so check Amazon's current pages directly rather than relying on any third-party description, this one included.

Do Amazon Partner Awards mean an agency is good at DSP?

An award tells you that Amazon judged specific work strong in a specific category in a specific year. That is real and checkable. It does not describe the firm's current programmatic team, its measurement method, or its fees, so use it as supporting evidence rather than as a decision.

What if a partner has no reviews and no awards?

Common, and not disqualifying. Switch to evidence you can generate yourself: published method, senior team tenure, portfolio figures with scope attached, and reference calls including one client who left. That file will be more relevant to your brief than any review corpus.

How many reference calls are enough?

Two per candidate, plus one former client if you can get it. Match on size and category rather than on enthusiasm — a glowing reference from a brand ten times your spend tells you about a service tier you will not receive.
