The short answer

Sponsored Brands are keyword-targeted ads that put your logo, a headline, and either a video or a set of products at the top of Amazon search results and the top of the home page. They run on CPC or vCPM pricing, with no minimum spend, sending shoppers to your Store or a landing page.

What a Sponsored Brands ad actually is

Sponsored Brands is Amazon's top-of-search, brand-level ad format. Unlike Sponsored Products, which puts a single ASIN into the results grid, Sponsored Brands puts your logo, a custom headline, and either a lineup of products or a video across the top of search results, inside search results, or at the top of the Amazon home page. Click it, and the shopper lands on your Brand Store, a custom landing page, or a product page — your choice.

It sits between Sponsored Products (bottom-of-funnel, ASIN-level) and Sponsored Display (audience-level, runs off-Amazon too). Sponsored Brands is the format built to answer "who makes this" and "what else do they sell," not just "which listing wins this click."

Three formats exist today: Product Collection (headline plus a lineup of products), Store Spotlight (headline plus up to three subpages of your Brand Store), and video (6–45 second autoplay clip). All three run under the same campaign type but use different creative slots and, for video, a different auction entirely.

How pricing actually works — a worked example

Sponsored Brands runs on two pricing models: cost-per-click (CPC), where you pay when someone clicks, and vCPM, where you pay per thousand viewable impressions. There's no official minimum spend, though Amazon's own guidance recommends at least $10 a day so the ad has a realistic shot at showing throughout the day. You set a bid and a daily budget; the more competitive the bid, the more often it clears the auction for a matching search.

Here's the mechanic, worked through with round numbers: say you bid $1.10 CPC against a $40 daily budget. If the ad clears the auction consistently, that's roughly 36 clicks before the budget caps for the day — fewer if competition pushes your effective CPC up, occasionally more because Amazon allows some daily overdelivery. What it actually holds you to is the monthly average daily spend, not any single day's number. That's the detail advertisers miss when they panic over one high-spend day and pause a campaign that's pacing fine.

vCPM works the same logic in reverse: you're buying guaranteed viewable delivery, not clicks, which fits video and pure-awareness placements better than a product-collection ad, where the click is the whole point.

The three formats, side by side

Which format fits depends on what you're trying to show, not just what you're trying to sell. A cookware brand like HexClad is a natural fit for video — a pan responding to heat is worth more as thirty seconds of demonstration than as a static image. A grooming brand with a built-out, multi-category Store, the kind Beardbrand runs, is a better fit for Store Spotlight than for a single product collection.

Sponsored Brands video: the auction most people don't realize is separate

Video runs in its own placement and its own auction. Practically, that means a Sponsored Brands video campaign doesn't compete against your other self-service Sponsored Brands or Sponsored Products campaigns for the same impression. It's genuinely incremental inventory by design, not cannibalized inventory — one of the few places in Amazon's ad stack where that's true structurally rather than something you have to prove with measurement.

Video clicks go straight to the product detail page, not the Store, so if the point is to tell a brand story before asking for the sale, that story has to land in the first few seconds. Autoplay is silent by default; if the video needs sound to make its point, it won't make it to most viewers.

Report on video the way you'd report on any awareness placement: impressions and view-through rate first, click and conversion metrics second. Judging a fifteen-second brand video on ACOS the day it launches is judging it on the wrong scale.

New-to-brand isn't the same as incremental — the mistake that costs the most

Sponsored Brands reporting includes a new-to-brand metric: the share of orders from shoppers who hadn't bought the brand in the past year. It's a genuinely useful number for a brand-awareness format. It is not proof the ad caused the sale — it's proof the buyer was new, which isn't the same claim. Last-click attribution will credit a Sponsored Brands ad for a sale a shopper was already three-quarters of the way toward making anyway.

This is where advertisers running Sponsored Brands alongside DSP get into real trouble: both systems will happily claim the same purchase, and neither console has a setting that stops it. You have to reconcile the two — in Amazon Marketing Cloud, or you're just adding two exaggerated numbers together and calling it strategy. We see this constantly on the DSP side. Across 30 advertisers in reMKTR's July 2026 book, the portfolio ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 CPC, with a $5.49 blended cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them new to the brand. None of that is a Sponsored Brands number — it's a different channel entirely. It's here because the lesson is the same one: a blended number across a whole book tells you something a cherry-picked line item never will, and the only way to know what a channel actually added is a holdout or a matched control, not a bigger last-click figure.

Mistakes that actually cost money, and what to check when the number is bad news

The most common mistake: writing the same headline logic for Sponsored Brands as for Sponsored Products, so the two most expensive placements on the search page say the identical thing. Sponsored Products can win on price and reviews alone. Sponsored Brands is the one placement where a shopper sees your logo before a competitor's ASIN — waste that on a generic "Shop Now" headline and you've paid a premium for nothing extra.

The second: pointing every click at the Store homepage instead of the subpage or landing page the ad actually promised. If the creative sold a bundle, land on the bundle page. We've made a version of this mistake ourselves — running Sponsored Brands creative that hadn't been updated to match a Store redesign, so the ad and the landing page told two different stories about what the brand sold. The ad and the Store live in different parts of the console; that's exactly why the gap is easy to miss.

  • Ad rejected on submission: almost always creative text or an unsupported claim, not targeting. Check the logo file and any claim that isn't backed on the product page — that's the most common rejection reason.
  • CTR is fine but conversion is flat: the landing page doesn't match the ad's promise. Fix the destination before touching the bid.
  • Budget won't spend: the bid is too low for the placement, or the keyword list is too narrow — Sponsored Brands needs broader keyword coverage than Sponsored Products to spend a meaningful daily budget.
  • New-to-brand looks great but sales aren't growing: you're likely capturing brand searches that would have converted anyway. Test with a holdout before crediting the ad.

Where Sponsored Brands fits with everything else you run

Sponsored Brands does one job well: it puts your brand, not just your ASIN, in front of a shopper already searching your category. It won't tell you whether that shopper would have found you anyway, and it won't tell you whether the sale it's claiming credit for is the same sale your Sponsored Display or DSP campaign is also claiming. That reconciliation problem doesn't go away because you switched ad formats — it's structural to running any two Amazon ad products at once with last-click reporting.

If you run Sponsored Brands yourself and never touch DSP, none of that applies to you — this is a self-service product that works fine without an agency. It's just worth knowing the reconciliation problem exists the day a second channel gets added.

Which one you should actually pick

Sponsored Brands suits Brand Registry sellers, vendors, and agencies who want top-of-search visibility for a brand story, a bundle, or a Store — not a single ASIN's price fight. It's weaker for commodity categories with little real differentiation, and its new-to-brand number needs a holdout before anyone treats it as proof of incrementality.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What's the difference between Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Products?

Sponsored Products advertises a single ASIN and competes on price, reviews, and relevance at the listing level. Sponsored Brands advertises the brand itself — logo, headline, and a curated set of products or a video — and can send shoppers to a Store instead of straight to checkout.

Do I need a Brand Store to run Sponsored Brands?

Only for the Store Spotlight format, and only if the Store has at least three subpages. Product Collection and video ads can land on a product detail page or a standalone landing page instead, so you can run Sponsored Brands without a Store built out.

How long should a Sponsored Brands video be?

Between 6 and 45 seconds, autoplay, muted by default. Put the point of the video in the first few seconds since most viewers won't turn on sound, and remember clicks go to the product page, not the Store.

Is there a minimum budget for Sponsored Brands?

No official minimum. Amazon recommends budgeting at least $10 a day so the ad has a realistic chance of showing throughout the day rather than exhausting itself in the first hour.

Does a high new-to-brand percentage mean Sponsored Brands is driving incremental sales?

Not on its own. New-to-brand tells you the buyer hadn't purchased in the past year — it doesn't tell you whether the ad caused the purchase or just took credit for one that would have happened anyway. Proving that requires a holdout or matched control, not a bigger last-click number.
