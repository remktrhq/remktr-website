Amazon Advertising Strategy 2022: The Framework That Never Expired

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1472 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon advertising strategy 2022”
The short answer

A 2022-style Amazon ad strategy still works because it was never about the year. It's a funnel: Sponsored Products for conversion, Sponsored Brands and Display for consideration, DSP for awareness and retargeting, all measured by incrementality rather than last-click credit.

Why 'the 2022 strategy' is the wrong question to ask

Search for this and you'll find guides that promise a 2022 playbook, then quietly re-date the same content every January. That's because the underlying structure of Amazon advertising hasn't changed since Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display matured: you still have three sponsored ad types plus DSP, and you still need all four working together, not just the one that's easiest to turn on.

What has changed is the tooling around that structure — Amazon Marketing Cloud now lets advertisers see DSP and sponsored ads without double-counting the same shopper twice, AI-assisted bidding has replaced a lot of manual rule-setting, and connected TV inventory through DSP is a real line item now, not a pilot. The strategy question isn't 'what's new for this year' — it's 'is my funnel actually a funnel, or is it just Sponsored Products with two afterthoughts bolted on.'

The funnel, stage by stage

Every durable Amazon advertising strategy, in 2022 or now, maps to four jobs. Skipping a stage doesn't save money — it just moves the cost to a stage that's worse at doing that job.

  • Awareness: DSP display, online video, audio and CTV reach people who haven't searched for you yet. This is the stage most self-serve sellers skip entirely, then wonder why new-to-brand purchases stall.
  • Consideration: Sponsored Brands headline placements and Sponsored Display audience retargeting keep your name in front of someone who's browsed but not bought.
  • Conversion: Sponsored Products, run against exact and broad match, does the closing. This is where most budgets live, and rightly so — but it can't do the other three jobs by itself.
  • Retention: DSP retargeting and Sponsored Display audience lists bring repeat buyers back without paying full search-term rates every time.

A worked example: what the numbers actually look like at scale

Numbers quoted in isolation are almost always the best line item, not the account. Across 30 advertisers reMKTR managed in July 2026, the portfolio delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole book, not cherry-picked from the best line item. That distinction matters more than the ratio itself: any account has a campaign that returns 15x. The question is what the whole thing does.

That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. If you've seen a $0.41 CPC quoted for this category elsewhere, that figure is online-video only — it isn't the blended number across search, display, and video together, and quoting it as the whole book is a common way strategy advice goes wrong. Blended cost per acquisition across that book was $5.49 on 57,137 attributed purchases, with 20.1% of those purchases coming from a shopper new to the brand. That new-to-brand share is the number that tells you whether the awareness stage is doing anything at all — if it's near zero, your top of funnel isn't a funnel, it's decoration.

The attribution mistake that undoes the whole strategy

Last-click attribution gives every sale to whichever ad the shopper clicked last, regardless of what actually moved them to buy. It was never built to answer the question strategy actually depends on: did this display impression cause an incremental sale, or did the shopper buy anyway and the ad just happened to be in the way?

Holdout tests and matched controls answer that question; last-click attribution cannot, and never could. Reconciling DSP and sponsored ads in Amazon Marketing Cloud is what stops the two channels from claiming credit for the same shopper — without that step, a brand can look like it's getting a great return from display when it's actually just re-billing sponsored ads' conversions under a different report.

What to do when the numbers look wrong

A strategy document is only useful if it says what to do when the dashboard disagrees with the plan. Before assuming the strategy failed, check these in order:

  • ACoS looks high on a new product: normal for the first few weeks. Compare against impression share lost to budget, not against a mature product's baseline.
  • DSP ROAS looks weak in isolation: check whether it's being measured last-click. Display's job is often to lift search performance downstream, not to close the sale itself — that only shows up in a holdout test or an AMC path-to-conversion view.
  • Spend isn't moving despite budget increases: check impression share lost to rank, not just lost to budget. More budget on a losing bid doesn't buy more impressions.
  • Search term report is full of irrelevant clicks: this is a targeting problem, not a bidding problem. Fix the negative keyword list before touching bids.

One honest mistake worth naming: pulling DSP the first time a monthly ROAS dips, because it's the easiest line to cut. We've done this ourselves early in an account before the holdout data existed to show the display spend was protecting search performance the whole time. It's the fastest way to make a strategy look right on paper for one month and worse for the next six.

Which one you should actually pick

Sellers running a single product line can execute this framework themselves inside Seller Central. Brands running DSP alongside sponsored ads at real budget need someone reconciling the two in AMC, or they're making decisions on numbers that double-count. That reconciliation, not the framework itself, is the part worth paying someone else to run.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is a 2022 Amazon advertising strategy still valid now?

The structure is — funnel stages built from Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display, and DSP haven't changed. What's dated is any specific bid tactic, keyword trick, or attribution window advice from that year. Rebuild the tactics, keep the framework.

How much of my budget should go to DSP versus sponsored ads?

There's no fixed rule, but a starting point of 15-25% on DSP for awareness and retention, with the rest split across sponsored ad types weighted toward conversion, is a reasonable default. Adjust based on whether you're launching a product (more conversion weight) or defending share against competitors (more retention weight).

Should I trust ACoS or ROAS as my main KPI?

Both are last-click metrics, so both share the same blind spot: neither tells you whether the sale would have happened anyway. Use ACoS and ROAS for day-to-day efficiency tracking, but validate the strategy periodically with a holdout test or matched-control comparison, which is the only way to see incremental impact rather than credit-claiming.

What's the single most common mistake in Amazon ad strategy?

Treating Sponsored Products as the whole strategy because it's the easiest to launch and the fastest to show a clean last-click ROAS. It closes sales but doesn't build the awareness that produces new-to-brand buyers, which is the number that actually predicts long-term growth.

What should I do if my Amazon ads suddenly stop converting?

Check impression share lost to budget versus lost to rank before touching bids — they need different fixes. Then check the search term report for irrelevant traffic. Only after ruling those out should you assume the audience or the listing itself is the problem.
