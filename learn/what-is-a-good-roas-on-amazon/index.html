What ROAS means on Amazon

ROAS is Return on Ad Spend: total ad-attributed sales divided by total ad spend. Amazon's own ad console reports it next to ACOS, which is the same relationship flipped: ACOS is spend divided by sales, ROAS is sales divided by spend. Spend $200, get $800 in attributed sales, and your ROAS is 4x (and your ACOS is 25%). Amazon explains the metric in its return on ad spend guide.

On its own, that number tells you nothing about whether the campaign made money. A 4x ROAS is excellent on a product with a healthy margin and a loss on one with almost nothing left after Amazon's referral and fulfillment fees. That's the gap every generic benchmark misses.

Why a quoted industry average isn't the answer

You'll see blanket averages quoted a lot, for example in Jungle Scout's guide to a good Amazon ROAS. They're not wrong exactly; they just answer a different question. A 3x ROAS could be deeply profitable for a supplement brand with a 70% margin and a loss-maker for a private-label kitchen tool with a 20% margin after COGS and Amazon fees. The average tells you what other people got. It doesn't tell you what you need.

What you need is your break-even ROAS: the point at which ad spend exactly eats your profit and no more. Below it, every ad-driven sale loses money. Above it, every sale contributes.

How to find your own minimum ROAS

Start from profit before advertising, not the sale price. For example, take a product that sells for $40. Cost of goods is $12. Amazon referral and FBA fees run $10. That leaves $18 of profit before you spend a cent on ads.

Minimum ROAS = sale price ÷ profit before ad spend. Here that's $40 ÷ $18 = 2.22x. Spend enough to hit exactly 2.22x and you've broken even. The same break-even as ACOS is $18 ÷ $40 = 45%. Anything above 2.22x is real margin; anything below it, you're funding growth out of your own pocket, which is sometimes worth it for a launch as long as you know you're doing it.

  • Higher margin products can tolerate a lower ROAS and still be profitable.
  • Lower margin products need a much higher ROAS just to break even, in some cases 6x, 8x, or more.
  • Your target ROAS is a business number, not an ad-platform default. Amazon's dashboard won't tell you what yours should be.

Once you have the break-even number, set your target above it. How far above depends on the goal: a profit-first campaign on proven keywords sits well above break-even, while a launch or new-to-brand push can deliberately sit at or just under it.

A quick checklist to set your own expected ROAS

Run this once per product, not once per account, because margins differ by ASIN:

  • Write down the sale price you actually receive after coupons and deals.
  • Subtract cost of goods, referral fees and FBA fees to get profit before ad spend.
  • Divide price by that profit to get break-even ROAS.
  • Decide the campaign's job: defending branded search, harvesting proven keywords, or buying new customers. Each earns a different target.
  • Re-run it when a cost changes: a fee increase, a new supplier price or a deal price moves your break-even.

What a good ROAS looks like once you're spending at scale

Single-campaign ROAS gets noisy fast, especially once you add upper-funnel DSP alongside Sponsored Products. Awareness placements such as online video, non-endemic display and prospecting audiences are supposed to run a lower ROAS than bottom-of-funnel search. That's not underperformance, that's the job the placement is doing. Judging it against a Sponsored Products benchmark is comparing a fishing net to a spear.

Across 30 advertisers we manage, July 2026 delivered a 6.04x return on ad spend measured across the whole portfolio, not the best line item. That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 CPC, a $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand. The $0.41 CPC you'll see quoted elsewhere in this category is online-video only, not the blended number, and not comparable to a search-heavy account.

The point isn't that 6.04x is the good number, and it isn't a promise about your account. It's that a portfolio number mixing prospecting and conversion will always read differently than a single search campaign, and any 'good ROAS' figure that doesn't say which mix it's measuring is telling you less than it looks like.

When your ROAS is bad news, here's what to actually check

A ROAS below your break-even number isn't automatically a crisis, and one above it isn't automatically a win. Check these before you touch bids:

  • Attribution window mismatch. Sponsored ads and DSP can both claim credit for the same sale if you're reading dashboards separately instead of reconciling them, so you double-count and think you're doing better than you are.
  • Last-click isn't incrementality. A display impression with a last-click sale attached doesn't prove the display caused the sale: the shopper might have converted from an organic search click regardless. A holdout test is the way to find out.
  • Cutting spend to defend ROAS. Pulling back to only your highest-converting, most branded keywords will lift the ratio and shrink the business at the same time. A rising ROAS on a shrinking sales number is not a win.
  • Wrong benchmark for the placement. Judging a retargeting or prospecting campaign against a branded-search ROAS will always look like failure, because it's answering a different question.

Which one you should actually pick

If you're running your own Sponsored Products account, Amazon's console plus your own break-even math is genuinely enough, and no vendor is needed. If you're adding DSP and want to know whether the display spend did anything beyond what search would have delivered anyway, that's a reconciliation and incrementality question, which is the part reMKTR builds its managed Amazon DSP service around.

What to do with this

Whoever runs your DSP, a partner or your own team, ask them two things: how much of DSP's attributed sales also had a sponsored ads click, and how they would hold out part of the audience in month one. Back whoever answers with a method rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What ROAS should I expect from Amazon advertising?

Expect whatever clears your own break-even ROAS, which is sale price divided by profit before ad spend. In the $40 example above that's 2.22x. A generic average can't replace that calculation, because margins differ so much between products.

Is a good ROAS different for Sponsored Products versus Sponsored Display?

Generally yes. Sponsored Products targets people already searching for your product or close substitutes, so it tends to convert at a higher rate and post a higher ROAS. Sponsored Display often does prospecting or retargeting work, so it's supposed to run lower: it's reaching people earlier in the decision, not closing an existing intent.

Should I use ROAS or ACOS to judge my campaigns?

They're the same relationship in different units. ACOS is spend divided by sales, ROAS is sales divided by spend, so 25% ACOS is 4x ROAS. Neither is more correct; use whichever your team thinks in. What matters is comparing either one against your break-even number, not against a generic industry figure.

My ROAS dropped even though I didn't change anything. What happened?

Check for attribution window changes, new competitor bidding pushing up your CPCs, or a shift in your sales mix toward lower-priced ASINs, which lowers ROAS even at a flat ad spend. Also rule out double-counting if you're running both sponsored ads and DSP and reading their dashboards separately instead of reconciled.

Is a low ROAS always a problem?

No. A launch campaign, a new-to-brand push, or a prospecting DSP line item can carry a lower ROAS by design and still be worth running, if the business goal is reach or new customers rather than immediate margin. The question is whether the lower ROAS is buying something you actually wanted.
