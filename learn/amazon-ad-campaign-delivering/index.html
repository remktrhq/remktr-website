Your Campaign Says "Delivering" — What That Actually Means

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1363 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon ad campaign delivering”
The short answer

Delivering means Amazon approved your campaign and it's eligible to show ads in auctions — it does not mean anyone has seen or clicked it yet. Sponsored ads charge only on click; DSP charges per impression. Zero spend with a delivering status usually just means you haven't won an auction yet.

What "delivering" actually means

Amazon's own definition is narrow: Delivering means the campaign is active and eligible to serve impressions — your ads will display in eligible placements when they win an auction for a relevant search or placement. That's it. It says nothing about how many people have seen it, clicked it, or bought anything.

People confuse "delivering" with "live," but Amazon doesn't use "live" as a status at all — that word comes from sellers, not the platform. The confusion matters because it changes what you should expect to see charged. Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display bill on click. If nobody has clicked, you owe nothing, even if the campaign has been delivering for hours. DSP line items bill on impression (CPM), so a delivering DSP campaign accrues cost the moment it serves — there's no click required for spend to move.

Why delivering with $0 spent is normal — a worked example

Say you launch a Sponsored Products campaign with a $4.00 CPM-equivalent bid on a mid-volume keyword. In the first few hours you might get 400 impressions and zero clicks. That's not broken — a 0% click rate over a few hundred impressions is well within normal variance for a new campaign still finding its placements.

Scale changes the picture, not the mechanics. Across 30 advertiser accounts we managed in July 2026, the book ran 78.4 million impressions at a blended $4.00 CPM and a $1.42 cost-per-click — and that blended CPC hides real range underneath it. The $0.41 CPC that gets quoted for this category everywhere is online-video only, not the whole book. Search and display placements run far higher than that. So a keyword sitting at "delivering, no clicks yet" for a day isn't a sign of failure; it may just be a slower lane sitting next to a much cheaper one in the same campaign.

When delivering status is actually bad news

Delivering is a green light, not a guarantee, and it can mask real problems:

  • Bid too low to win auctions. Status reads delivering, but you're never clearing the floor for competitive placements — check the bid landscape tool, not just the status page.
  • Budget exhausted early in the day. The campaign delivered fine for two hours, then silently stopped once the daily budget ran out. Status can lag reality here by several hours.
  • Old creative still running. If you edited an ad and the new version is pending review, Amazon keeps the previously approved version delivering — you may be paying for a version you thought you'd replaced.
  • DSP frequency capping. A DSP line item can say delivering while serving almost nothing to a given audience because it's already hit the frequency cap for that user pool.

The honest rule: if a campaign hasn't produced a single impression in 48–72 hours despite an approved ad and a reasonable bid, something is wrong. If it's producing impressions but no clicks, look at the bid and the placement report before touching anything else.

The mistakes people make with this status — including ours

The most common seller mistake is panic-pausing a campaign the moment it shows delivering with no spend, before 24 hours have even passed. Amazon's status can lag actual delivery by up to a day, so pausing early just resets the auction-learning clock and starts the delay over.

The mistake we've made, and see other DSP operators make constantly: treating a delivering status and a rising impression count as proof the campaign is working. It isn't proof of anything on its own. Last-click attribution tells you a sale happened near an ad — it cannot tell you whether that sale would have happened anyway. The only way to know if delivering impressions actually moved incremental sales is a holdout or matched control, not a bigger impression number. Common questions

My campaign has been delivering for two days with zero clicks — is that normal?

It depends on volume and bid. On a low-search-volume keyword with a modest bid, a few days without a click can happen even when everything is set up correctly. If impressions aren't accumulating at all in that window, the problem is more likely a bid or budget issue than bad luck — check the bid landscape before assuming the keyword is dead.

Does delivering mean my ad is winning every auction it enters?

No. Delivering only means the campaign is eligible to enter auctions. It can lose most of them and still show as delivering, because the status describes eligibility, not win rate. Impression share and placement reports show you the win rate; the status page does not.

Is 'delivering' the same thing in Amazon DSP as it is in Sponsored Products?

The word is the same but the billing behind it isn't. Sponsored ads only charge when a shopper clicks, so a delivering campaign with no clicks costs nothing. DSP charges per impression served, so a delivering DSP line item is spending money the moment it shows an ad, whether or not anyone clicks.

Can a campaign show delivering but actually be running an old, disapproved version of my ad?

Yes. If you submit a creative edit and it's still pending review, Amazon keeps serving the last approved version rather than pausing the campaign. The status stays delivering throughout, which can be confusing if you assumed your change had already gone live.

How do I know if a delivering campaign is actually driving incremental sales, not just sales that would have happened anyway?

You can't tell from the status page or from last-click attribution alone — both will look identical whether the campaign added sales or just took credit for ones that were coming regardless. A holdout group or matched control is the only reliable way to separate the two, which is why reconciling DSP and sponsored ads together in one measurement layer matters more than watching either dashboard in isolation.
