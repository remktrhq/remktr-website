Sales Rank, Star Rating, or Search Rank — Which One Do You Mean?

People searching "Amazon sales rating" usually mean one of two different metrics, and mixing them up leads to bad decisions.

Best Sellers Rank (BSR) is a live number based purely on units sold, updated frequently, and specific to a category. Star rating is the 1-to-5 average from customer reviews. A product can have a great star rating and a terrible BSR, or the reverse. Neither one measures the other.

There's a third number people confuse with both: organic search rank, meaning where a product shows up when a shopper types a keyword. Amazon's own help pages state that a top BSR doesn't guarantee a high search position, and a high search position isn't guaranteed to carry a high BSR. Both correlate loosely with sales volume, but neither causes the other.

How Best Sellers Rank Is Calculated (With a Worked Example)

BSR is calculated from sales volume, weighted so recent sales count more than older sales, and it resets separately for every category and subcategory a product is assigned to. A product ranked #4 in "Cast Iron Skillets" might sit at #38,000 in the parent "Home & Kitchen" category, simply because the subcategory has a few thousand listings and the parent has millions.

Worked example: say a product sells 40 units on a given day, in a subcategory where the next four competitors sold 55, 48, 44, and 41 units that same day. That product lands at rank #5 in that day's snapshot. Sell 10 more units tomorrow while competitors hold flat, and the rank climbs — not because anything on the listing changed, but because the sales count did. That's why BSR swings daily even when the page itself hasn't been touched.

Categories full of close competitors selling at similar volume — cookware is a good example, the kind of category a brand like HexClad competes in — see BSR move hard on small day-to-day sales differences. Long-tail categories with mostly low-volume listings barely move at all.

When the Number Looks Wrong, or the Fix Didn't Work

If a BSR looks wrong, or a fix you made didn't move it, check these before assuming the metric is broken:

  • It's lagging, not wrong. BSR updates frequently but not instantly — a sales spike from last night may not show for hours.
  • Parent/child ASIN mismatch. Product variations can carry different BSRs if sales aren't rolling up to the parent the way you expect.
  • Category misassignment. A product can rank well in the wrong subcategory and it means nothing — confirm the category is one buyers actually browse.
  • One good day isn't a trend. BSR is a snapshot. A single high-velocity day — a coupon, a deal, a spike in clicks — can move it temporarily, and it drifts back once volume normalizes.
  • Advertising doesn't move BSR directly. It only moves BSR by moving units sold. A campaign that drives clicks but not purchases won't touch it.

The Mistake Advertisers Make: Watching BSR to Judge Whether Ads Worked

The most common mistake we see: a brand runs a DSP or sponsored ads campaign, watches BSR improve during the campaign window, and credits the ads. BSR moved because units sold moved. It doesn't tell you whether those units would have sold anyway, or whether the impressions actually reached someone who wasn't already going to buy.

Last-click attribution has the same blind spot at a bigger scale — it can't separate an incremental sale from one that was coming regardless. That's why we reconcile in Amazon Marketing Cloud instead of relying on last click, and why holdouts and matched controls, not rank charts, are what actually test whether display added anything.

What that looks like in practice: across 30 advertisers we ran in July 2026, the portfolio delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole book, not cherry-picked from the best line item — with a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases came from shoppers new to the brand. None of that shows up in a BSR chart. BSR tells you units moved. It can't tell you why.

Star Rating: A Separate System That Still Matters

Star rating runs on a completely separate mechanism: the average of submitted customer reviews, weighted by Amazon's own recency and verified-purchase logic. Amazon states plainly that BSR isn't influenced by page views or customer reviews. The two systems don't feed each other directly.

Where they connect is indirectly, through conversion. A low star rating can suppress conversion rate, which lowers units sold, which lowers BSR over time. But that's a second-order effect through sales, not a direct rule linking the two numbers. The best seller badge has its own separate threshold — it only appears on a product ranked #1 in a category with at least 100 products.

Where This Leaves You

BSR and star rating are both public, free, and useful for a quick read on how a listing is doing — no tool required. Where they run out of road is proving whether a specific ad dollar caused a specific sale. Which one you should actually pick

For a quick, free read on listing health, BSR and star rating tell you plenty and need no tool. For proving an ad dollar caused an incremental sale, they tell you nothing — that question needs holdout or clean-room measurement, which is a different job entirely.

Common questions

Does advertising improve my Amazon sales rating?

Only indirectly. Ads improve BSR by driving purchases, not through any direct mechanism. A campaign that drives clicks without conversions won't move BSR at all, and Amazon states BSR isn't influenced by page views.

Why does my BSR jump around so much day to day?

Because it's a snapshot of recent sales within your category. Small day-to-day sales swings look large in categories with a handful of close competitors selling at similar volume.

What counts as a good BSR?

There's no fixed number — it depends entirely on how many listings are in that category or subcategory. A #500 rank in a category with 50,000 listings is strong; the same number in a category with 2,000 listings is not.

Does a bad star rating hurt my BSR directly?

No direct link, per Amazon's own documentation. The indirect path is through conversion: a low rating can suppress the purchase rate, which lowers units sold, which lowers BSR over time.

Can I use BSR to prove my ads worked?

No. BSR only shows that units sold changed, not whether the ad caused that change or the sale would have happened anyway. Proving that requires a holdout test or matched-control measurement, not a rank chart.
