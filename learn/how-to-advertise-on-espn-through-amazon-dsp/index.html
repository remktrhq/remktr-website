How to Advertise on ESPN Through Amazon DSP

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1449 words · Written against what currently ranked for “How to advertise on ESPN through Amazon DSP”
The short answer

ESPN's inventory became buyable through Amazon DSP as part of the Disney-Amazon DRAX integration announced 17 June 2025, alongside Disney+ and Hulu. It runs on the same DSP setup as any other supply source, with pricing that skews toward the premium end because it is live-sports content — and it complements, rather than duplicates, Amazon's own Thursday Night Football rights.

ESPN's route into Amazon DSP

ESPN reaches Amazon DSP through the same channel as Disney+ and Hulu: the DRAX integration Amazon Ads and Disney Advertising announced on 17 June 2025, with full access for US Amazon DSP advertisers arriving in the third quarter of that year. There is no ESPN-specific application — if your DSP seat can already reach Disney+ and Hulu, it can reach ESPN, selected as its own supply source inside the same campaign structure.

What makes ESPN worth handling separately from Disney's other two properties is that it is live content, not on-demand. Delivery is concentrated around live windows rather than spread evenly across a month the way on-demand streaming impressions are, and cost typically follows that concentration — inventory around a marquee live event is priced and contested differently than inventory around a rerun.

How this sits next to Amazon's own Thursday Night Football

It's worth being precise about what's actually two separate things. Amazon owns the exclusive US rights to Thursday Night Football through the 2033 season — that's Amazon-owned live-sports inventory, sold as part of Amazon's own streaming ad product. ESPN's live sports inventory is a completely different rights holder's content, reachable through the Disney DRAX connection. The practical result for a media planner is that Amazon DSP is now one of the few places you can buy into both of the two biggest live-sports environments in US streaming from a single seat, with a single frequency cap governing both if you choose to run them together.

That doesn't mean they behave the same. TNF is a single weekly national event with a fixed, predictable calendar; ESPN's live-sports slate is broader and more varied across sports and days, which generally gives more flexibility for testing smaller budgets against specific games rather than committing to one weekly national window.

For a sports-adjacent brand this pairing is genuinely useful: it means a plan doesn't have to choose one rights holder over the other, and can instead build reach around whichever specific matchups are relevant to the audience — a running-shoe brand around marathon-adjacent programming on ESPN, an outdoor brand around a Thursday primetime game with broad national reach, or both at once inside one budget.

Setting up an ESPN buy

Select ESPN as a distinct supply source rather than leaving a campaign set to the whole Disney bundle, for the same reason that applies to Disney+ and Hulu: a blended line item makes it impossible to tell which property is actually delivering your results. Creative specs follow the general envelope Amazon publishes for DSP video — 16:9, minimum 1920×1080 frame — and because ESPN inventory sits around live content, plan for less flexibility in exact placement timing than you'd get with on-demand video, where delivery can pace evenly across 30 days.

Rollout for the whole DRAX bundle, ESPN included, reached full access for all US Amazon DSP advertisers in the third quarter of 2025, after Disney+ launched first outside the US in a smaller set of markets. If ESPN isn't showing as an available supply source at all, that's worth checking against the rollout timeline before assuming a configuration error on your side.

A worked example

Set aside $15,000 for a month that includes several marquee ESPN live windows. Premium content adjacency — inventory sitting next to appointment viewing rather than filler content — is reported in the industry at roughly $40–$65 CPM, well above the $20–$50 range quoted for premium streaming generally. At a representative $45 CPM, that buys around 333,000 impressions. Compare that delivery against a top-of-funnel target: across 27 advertisers in our own Amazon DSP book over a 31-day window this summer, connected-TV and streaming inventory overall returned 0.77x on strict last-click attribution while carrying the highest new-to-brand rate on the report, 56.3%. Live-sports inventory specifically skews even further toward reach and unattributed influence than on-demand streaming does, so judge it primarily on completion rate and branded search movement, not on a same-week ROAS number.

What to do when it goes wrong

If ESPN delivery is thin around a specific game, check whether the budget was paced evenly across the month rather than weighted toward the actual live windows — an evenly-paced daily budget will underspend during a big game and overspend during a quiet one, missing the audience concentration that makes live sports valuable in the first place. If cost per impression spikes sharply during a marquee window, that is closer to expected than broken: every other advertiser targeting the same live event is bidding into the same limited inventory at once, the same dynamic that inflates DSP costs broadly during Amazon's own Prime Week. Plan the budget knowing the most valuable minutes will also be the most expensive ones.

The common mistake

The mistake is buying ESPN inventory the way you'd buy a evenly-delivered on-demand streaming campaign — flat daily budget, standard frequency cap, no adjustment for the fact that a live audience behaves completely differently than an on-demand one. A live sports audience is unusually attentive during the actual event and largely absent outside it; a plan that doesn't concentrate spend and creative timing around the live window is leaving the format's real advantage — a genuinely captive national audience at a known time — mostly on the table. reMKTR builds ESPN and TNF buys around the live calendar first, and lets the rest of the streaming plan fill the gaps between events.

Which one you should actually pick

ESPN through Amazon DSP suits a sports-adjacent brand, or any brand with a national reach goal, that wants live-event attention without committing to Amazon's own weekly Thursday Night Football window. A brand that needs guaranteed placement inside a specific broadcast, or one working with a budget too small to be meaningfully visible during a marquee live window, is better served elsewhere in the streaming plan.

Common questions

Do I need a separate ESPN or Disney account to run these ads?

No. ESPN is reachable as a supply source inside Amazon DSP through the same DRAX integration that opened Disney+ and Hulu, using the DSP seat you already have.

Is ESPN inventory more expensive than Disney+ or Hulu?

It tends to run higher, because it is live content, and live-event inventory generally commands a premium over on-demand streaming. Amazon does not publish a fixed rate card for any of the three, so treat any specific figure as auction-driven and current only to the day you read it.

Can I target a specific game or sport on ESPN through Amazon DSP?

Targeting granularity depends on what DRAX exposes at the time you set up the campaign — check current options in your own DSP console rather than assuming full game-level control, since live-sports programmatic targeting is generally coarser than on-demand content targeting.

Should I run ESPN and Thursday Night Football in the same campaign?

You can, and Amazon's authenticated graph will apply a household-level frequency cap across both if you set it up that way. Whether it's the right call depends on whether you want them measured together or want each property's contribution isolated — running them as separate line items under a shared cap gets you both.

Is ESPN inventory brand-safe for a consumer product?

Live sports content is generally considered a brand-safe environment for most consumer categories, though the usual creative-review process still applies to what your own ad says, not to the surrounding content — check Amazon's current ad policies rather than assuming clearance based on category alone.

What kind of budget do I need to see meaningful ESPN delivery?

Amazon's streaming TV product recommends a $10,000 minimum for a self-serve DSP campaign generally; against premium live-sports CPMs, a budget near that floor will buy a modest number of impressions concentrated around specific events rather than broad delivery across every live window in a month.
