Is It Worth Paying Extra to Capture Co-Viewing Data in a Campaign Report?

Updated November 13, 2026 · By Ken Freeman · 1531 words · Written against what currently ranked for “Co-viewing: why streaming reach is bigger than the impression count”
The short answer

Usually only if someone will act on it. Co-viewing data is worth paying for when you must report people reached, or compare streaming with per-person channels. For bidding and budget decisions it rarely changes anything, because the auction runs on impression counts. Until then, treat the impression count as a floor.

The short answer: pay when the number changes a decision

Co-viewing data estimates how many people sat in front of the screen for each ad serve. It is useful, but it is a reporting layer, not a buying lever. Ask one question before you pay: who will read this number, and what will they do differently because of it?

  • Worth paying for: a stakeholder or client is comparing your streaming reach against a channel that counts individual people, and the gap is making streaming look weak.
  • Worth paying for: you are setting a reach target in people, not impressions, and need the report to match it.
  • Not worth paying for: you are only deciding bids, budgets or creative inside the DSP. Those run on impressions, completion rate and downstream sales, none of which co-viewing data changes.
  • Not worth paying for: the report will be read as proof of results. Co-viewing adds estimated audience, not sales.

What co-viewing actually is

Co-viewing is the industry term for more than one person watching the same screen at the same time, such as a household watching a show together rather than each person on a separate device. It matters for streaming and connected-TV specifically because the ad-serving unit is the device or the authenticated household, not an individual tracked person the way a mobile app impression often is. One ad serve to a living-room TV during a co-viewed program can reach several people at once, and none of that additional reach shows up as additional impressions in a standard delivery report.

The phenomenon is not new to streaming. Broadcast TV always had the same property. What has changed is that streaming's device-level, authenticated targeting makes the underlying household identifiable in a way broadcast measurement never could, which is what makes better co-viewing methodology both possible and necessary now.

Why this is a live measurement question right now

Streaming is too large a share of viewing for this to stay a footnote. According to Nielsen's own published data, streaming captured a record 47.5% of total TV viewing in December 2025. Nielsen has separately been rolling out updated co-viewing measurement, layering a "Big Data + Panel" approach across tens of millions of households and folding further updates into its methodology from September 2026 onward, as Variety reported.

That matters for your decision in two ways. The effort to measure co-viewing more precisely is strong evidence it is not a rounding error. But the methodology is also still moving, which is a reason not to pay a premium today for a number that may be restated within months.

What this means for reading your own numbers

An impression count from Amazon DSP tells you how many times an ad was served to a device or household. It cannot tell you how many individual people were in the room. The reach and frequency figures in a standard DSP report are therefore conservative by construction: real exposure on shared-screen streaming inventory is very likely higher than the impression count implies, not lower. That cuts against the instinct to distrust CTV reach because it looks smaller than a brand's mental model of everyone watching TV.

It also fits what we see in our own accounts. Across 30 advertisers in our own Amazon DSP book in July 2026, 20.1% of attributed purchases came from shoppers new to the brand. That is a whole-book figure, not a streaming-only one, but it shows why delivery reports undersell upper-funnel work: a co-viewer who never generated their own tracked impression can still be the person who searches the brand later, invisible to the delivery report and present in the new-to-brand number the following week.

A worked example: is the add-on cost worth it?

This is an illustration, not a quote. Say a measurement add-on costs $2,000 a month and your streaming budget is $20,000 a month, so the report costs 10% on top of the media. Run three checks:

  • Will the number move budget? If the answer is no, you are paying $2,000 for a slide.
  • Is it a known method? Ask whether the co-viewing factor comes from a named, current methodology or from the vendor's own fixed multiplier. A single multiplier applied to every household and program is an assumption, not a measurement.
  • Is it separate from the delivery number? The estimate should sit beside the impression count, never replace it.

If the first answer is yes, for example because a client is about to cut streaming on a reach comparison, the add-on can pay for itself. If it is no, spend that $2,000 on media and read the impression count as a floor.

What to do instead, practically

Do not apply your own multiplier to impression counts. No single, publicly verified co-viewing ratio fits every household and content type, and Nielsen's own measurement is still being overhauled. Precise per-household ratios circulate in industry commentary; treat any confidently stated multiplier from a source other than a current, named methodology with real skepticism.

What is actionable costs nothing:

  • Label streaming reach as impressions or households in every report, and say plainly that people reached is likely higher.
  • When comparing against a single-viewer format like mobile in-app video, note that the two are not measured on the same basis.
  • Judge streaming on signals co-viewing does not touch: completion rate, branded search movement and new-to-brand rate.
  • If a stakeholder insists on a people-based figure, buy co-viewing data for that reporting need only, and keep the delivery report as the source of truth.

The common mistakes

The first mistake is treating a streaming impression count as a literal count of people reached, then comparing it unfavorably against a channel that tracks individuals directly. One number is a conservative floor and the other is closer to a precise count. reMKTR runs Amazon DSP as a service and presents streaming reach with that context attached rather than as a bare number sitting beside channels measured on different terms.

The opposite mistake is using co-viewing as an escape hatch for every weak result. It supports a structural argument about how impression counts should be read. It is not an excuse for a campaign with poor completion rate and no branded search movement; co-viewing does not affect those two signals, and they remain the honest read of whether the creative and targeting are working.

Which one you should actually pick

Pay for co-viewing data when a stakeholder is comparing streaming reach against per-person channels, or when your target is set in people, and ask which methodology sits behind it. Skip it if you are only optimizing bids and budget inside the DSP, where the impression count is still the number the auction runs on.

What to do with this

Whoever runs your DSP, a partner or your own team, ask them two things: how much of DSP's attributed sales also had a sponsored ads click, and how they would hold out part of the audience in month one. Back whoever answers with a method rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is it worth paying extra for co-viewing data in a campaign report?

Only when the number will change a decision, such as defending streaming against a per-person reach comparison. For bidding and budget decisions inside the DSP it rarely matters, so the money is usually better spent on media.

Is there a standard co-viewing multiplier I can apply to my impression counts?

No reliable, publicly verified figure applies across households and content types, and inventing one would be an unsupported number dressed up as a fact. Treat the point qualitatively: real reach is very likely higher than reported, not a specific multiple higher.

Does co-viewing affect frequency capping accuracy?

It can, in the sense that a cap set per authenticated household governs the device or account, not each individual viewer in the room. That is a limitation of household-level capping generally, not something that changes how the cap should be set.

Why is Nielsen changing its co-viewing measurement now?

Streaming has grown to nearly half of all TV viewing, which raises the stakes on measuring it accurately. Nielsen has been rolling out updated methodology, including a broader data-plus-panel approach, with further updates folded in from September 2026.

Does co-viewing apply to mobile and desktop video the same way it applies to CTV?

Generally no. CTV's living-room, shared-screen context is where co-viewing matters most. Mobile and desktop video are far more often single-viewer experiences, which is part of why the two should not be compared on impression count alone.
