What 'Amazon Marketing Online' Actually Means

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1593 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon marketing online”
The short answer

Amazon marketing online covers two different products: self-serve sponsored ads (cost-per-click, inside Amazon search) and Amazon DSP (managed programmatic display and video, on and off Amazon). Measured separately with last-click attribution, they overlap and double-count each other's credit for the same purchase.

The Two Layers Under 'Amazon Marketing'

Amazon marketing online gets used to mean two different things. One is sponsored ads inside Amazon's own search and product pages. The other is Amazon DSP, programmatic display and video bought through a demand-side platform, on and off Amazon. They aren't bought the same way, managed the same way, or measured the same way.

Sponsored ads (Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display) are self-serve. You set a budget, bid on keywords or products, pay per click, and there's no spend minimum to open an account. Amazon's own advertising site notes that going further — working with an Amazon Ads account executive on a managed-service basis — typically requires a minimum investment of USD 50,000. Below that line, most brands run sponsored ads themselves directly in the ad console.

DSP sits above that. It's a managed-service layer that buys display, video and audio inventory using shopping and streaming signals to target audiences rather than keywords, reaching people on Amazon and off it. Some brands run sponsored ads for the bottom of the funnel and DSP for everything above it — outdoor and grooming DTC names among them.

How the Numbers Actually Connect: A Worked Example

Definitions are easy to find. What's harder to find is how the numbers fit together once real spend is running. Here's one real book, not a hypothetical: across 30 advertisers reMKTR ran in July 2026, the portfolio logged 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, a blended $1.42 CPC, and a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, for a 6.04x return on ad spend measured across the whole book, not cherry-picked from the best line item.

Those numbers should reconcile with each other, and roughly do. 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM implies spend of roughly $313,600. Separately, 57,137 purchases at a $5.49 blended CPA implies spend of roughly $313,800. Two different ways of counting the same media land within a rounding error of each other — that's what tells you the measurement is honest rather than double-counted. Multiply that spend by the 6.04x ROAS and you get roughly $1.9M in attributed revenue for the period.

One more number worth sitting with: 20.1% of those 57,137 purchases came from a shopper new to the brand. That's the number that tells you whether media is expanding the customer file or just harvesting people who were going to buy anyway — and last-click attribution alone won't give it to you cleanly, because it doesn't separate new demand from existing demand.

The Format Ladder, From Self-Serve to Managed

Every format sold under 'Amazon marketing' sits on a ladder from fully self-serve to fully managed. The table below is the ladder, not a comparison of vendors — it's the same structure whether you run it yourself or hand it to an agency.

  • Sponsored Products: keyword or product targeting, cost-per-click, no minimum spend.
  • Sponsored Brands: keyword targeting with brand creative, cost-per-click, no minimum spend.
  • Sponsored Display: audience and product retargeting, cost-per-click or vCPM, no minimum spend.
  • Amazon DSP: programmatic display, video and audio, on and off Amazon, typically managed-service with a real spend floor.

The Mistake Almost Everyone Makes With the CPC Number

You'll see a $0.41 CPC quoted all over this category, presented as the cost per click for Amazon DSP. It's real, but it's not the whole book — it's the online-video line item, which prices differently than click-based display because it's completion-based. The blended figure across a full DSP book, ours included, comes out closer to $1.42. Quoting the low number without saying which tactic it belongs to is the single most common way this category misleads people, and it's a mistake we've had to correct in our own materials before.

The second common mistake is measuring DSP and sponsored ads with the same last-click attribution and comparing them as if they're interchangeable. Last-click credits whichever ad a shopper touched right before buying — usually a sponsored ad, because it sits at the bottom of the funnel by design. That doesn't mean an earlier DSP impression did nothing; it means last-click can't see it. Amazon Marketing Cloud, which de-duplicates DSP and sponsored ads exposure at the shopper level, is the tool that can actually show whether display spend added incremental purchases or just repeated a story the search ad was already going to close.

What To Do When the Number You're Looking At Is Wrong

Three situations come up constantly. First: your ROAS in the ad console looks fine but your P&L doesn't feel it. Check whether DSP and sponsored ads are being summed rather than de-duplicated — added together, the same purchase can get counted twice, once per channel. Second: CPA is climbing month over month with no change in targeting. That's usually audience saturation, not a broken campaign; the fix is expanding audience or placement mix, not raising bids on the same one. Third: new-to-brand share is falling. That's the earliest sign spend is drifting toward retargeting people who already know the brand — cheap on paper, but it stops growing the customer file.

None of these get fixed by staring at the dashboard harder. They get fixed by running a holdout — a matched group that sees no DSP exposure — and comparing purchase rate against the exposed group. That's the only test that answers whether the media added anything, versus which channel got credited for it.

Which one you should actually pick

Self-serve sponsored ads suit any brand running its own account below the managed-service spend floor — no minimum, full control, and Amazon's own tools are genuinely fine for this. Managed Amazon DSP suits brands already past that floor who need someone holding the seat, buying the media, and proving in Amazon Marketing Cloud whether the display spend is adding purchases or just getting credit for ones sponsored ads would have closed anyway — that's the layer reMKTR operates in.

Common questions

Is Amazon advertising the same thing as Amazon marketing?

No. Amazon marketing is broader — it includes listing content, images, pricing and organic ranking factors alongside paid media. Amazon advertising is the paid layer specifically: sponsored ads and Amazon DSP. Most people typing 'amazon marketing online' are actually asking about the paid layer.

Do I need Amazon DSP or just sponsored ads to start?

Sponsored ads have no spend minimum and are self-serve, so almost every brand starts there. Amazon DSP is structured as a managed-service product with a real spend floor — Amazon's own site cites a minimum investment around USD 50,000 for account-executive-managed service. If you're below that, sponsored ads alone is the right starting point.

Why doesn't my ROAS match between the ad console and my own numbers?

Usually attribution windows differ, or DSP and sponsored ads are being added together rather than de-duplicated, which can double-count the same purchase. Amazon Marketing Cloud is built specifically to remove that overlap and show a single, reconciled number.

What does 'new-to-brand' actually measure?

It's the share of purchases from shoppers who hadn't bought the brand in the prior lookback period. It matters because it separates media that's growing the customer file from media that's just re-billing people who were already going to buy — a distinction last-click attribution alone can't make cleanly.

Is the $0.41 CPC number people quote for Amazon DSP real?

It's real, but it's typically the online-video line item only, which prices on completion rather than click and runs cheaper than the blended average across a full DSP book. A blended figure across click-based display, video and other formats together usually comes out higher — closer to $1 or more per click.
