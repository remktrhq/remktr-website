How Amazon Advertising Lives Inside Seller Central

Amazon Advertising and Seller Central are not the same product, but Seller Central is where most sellers reach Amazon Advertising for the first time. Log into Seller Central, click Advertising in the top menu, and you land in Campaign Manager — the actual advertising console, hosted at advertising.amazon.com. Seller Central is the front door, not the workshop.

Access depends on what you're trying to run. A Professional selling plan is enough for Sponsored Products. Sponsored Brands, self-service Display, and free Brand Stores all require your account to be enrolled in Brand Registry as well. If you're not enrolled, those options either won't appear in the menu or will show an enrollment prompt — that's the single most common reason a seller thinks a feature is missing.

If you don't sell on Amazon at all, you can still register for Amazon Ads directly, without ever creating a Seller Central account — useful for brands running awareness campaigns off-Amazon, or vendors advertising through Vendor Central instead.

The Three Ad Types You Can Turn On From Seller Central

Everything self-service in this console sits on one pricing model: cost-per-click. You pay when someone clicks, not when they see the ad.

  • Sponsored Products — promotes one listing at a time in search results and on product pages. No Brand Registry needed, no minimum spend, no upfront fee. This is where almost every seller starts.
  • Sponsored Brands — a banner with your logo, a headline, and several products, running at the top of search. Priced on CPC or vCPM (cost per 1,000 viewable impressions). Requires Brand Registry.
  • Display — image or video ads that follow shoppers across Amazon's own inventory and some outside apps, using Amazon's audience data rather than keywords. Requires Brand Registry, available self-service or through a managed setup.

All three live in the same Campaign Manager console you reach from Seller Central's Advertising tab. None require a DSP seat, an account executive, or a minimum budget — that only shows up once you move past self-service.

A Worked Example: Turning a Budget Into Clicks and Orders

The math behind Sponsored Products is simple enough to do before you set a budget, not after.

Say you set a $50 daily budget and your bids average out to a $1 cost-per-click — a round number for illustration, since your real CPC depends entirely on category and competition. That's roughly 50 clicks a day. If 1 in 10 clicks converts, you get 5 orders a day from that campaign. Push the daily budget to $150 at the same CPC and conversion rate, and you're looking at roughly 15 orders a day — assuming the campaign doesn't run out of budget before the day ends, which is the part most dashboards won't flag in plain English.

That last caveat matters more than the arithmetic. A campaign that exhausts its budget by 11 a.m. every day isn't underperforming — it's under-funded. Seller Central will show you spend, clicks, and ACOS, but it won't tell you outright that you're leaving impressions on the table. You have to notice the pattern in the hourly data yourself.

Where Seller Central Ads Stop and Amazon DSP Starts

Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Display all run inside the console you reach from Seller Central. Amazon DSP does not. It's a separate buying platform tied to its own advertiser seat, and it prices on CPM rather than pure CPC, because DSP buys display and video inventory programmatically, including off-Amazon, not just search placements.

The CPC figure people quote for "Amazon advertising" often gets muddled between these two systems. We run 109 live DSP advertiser seats, and across 30 of those advertisers in July 2026 the book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. The $0.41 CPC that gets quoted around this category is an online-video-only number, not the blended reality across a full DSP book. Sponsored Products CPC, DSP video CPC, and blended DSP CPC are three different figures — treat any single number you see online with that in mind.

Amazon Ads' own FAQ states that managed-service advertising through an account executive — closer to how DSP typically gets sold — normally requires a minimum investment of USD $50,000. Self-service Sponsored Products has no such floor. That gap is the real dividing line between advertising in Seller Central and advertising through Amazon DSP.

When It's Not Working: Troubleshooting and the Attribution Mistake

Three things break more Sponsored Products campaigns than bad keywords ever do. Zero impressions usually means you're losing the Buy Box — a sponsored ad can be live, funded, and eligible and still show nothing if a competitor undercuts your price. Spend that flatlines mid-day means your daily budget hit its cap; check the hour it stopped, and if it's the same hour every day, the budget is the constraint, not the product. High ACOS with no sales lift is often a listing problem — a recent price change, a stockout, a suppressed Buy Box — showing up in ad numbers before it shows up anywhere else.

The other mistake is treating ACOS as the whole truth about whether an ad worked. Last-click attribution credits whichever ad a shopper clicked last, even if they'd already decided to buy from an organic search a day earlier. We've made a version of this mistake ourselves: early on, reading a DSP display campaign's last-click sales as incremental, when a chunk of those shoppers would have converted anyway. It's why we now reconcile DSP and sponsored ads together in Amazon Marketing Cloud instead of trusting either console's dashboard alone — a holdout or matched-control test shows what a display ad actually added; last-click structurally cannot, no matter how clean the report looks.

For Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands, this matters less, since those ads are usually the last click by design. It matters most once Display or DSP sits on top, where attribution stops being cosmetic and becomes the whole question.

Which one you should actually pick

Self-service Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands inside Seller Central suit sellers who want direct control with no minimum spend — most brands should run these themselves before paying anyone. Amazon DSP suits brands that already have sponsored ads dialed in and want to test reach beyond search, provided someone reconciles attribution instead of trusting last-click. reMKTR only fits that second case.

Common questions

Do I need Seller Central to advertise on Amazon?

If you sell on Amazon, yes — Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Display all connect through your Seller Central account and its Campaign Manager. If you don't sell on Amazon, you can register for Amazon Ads directly and run Display or DSP campaigns without ever creating a Seller Central account.

What's the difference between Sponsored Products and Amazon DSP?

Sponsored Products is self-service, keyword-based, cost-per-click, and lives inside the Seller Central advertising console. Amazon DSP is a separate programmatic buying platform priced on CPM, capable of reaching audiences off-Amazon, and typically sold through a managed-service relationship rather than a self-service signup.

Why can't I see Sponsored Brands or Display in my Seller Central Advertising tab?

Both require Brand Registry enrollment. Sponsored Products doesn't. If your account isn't enrolled, those options either won't display or will prompt you to enroll before you can build a campaign.

Is there a minimum budget for Amazon advertising?

Self-service Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Display have no published minimum — you set your own daily budget. Amazon Ads' own FAQ states that managed-service advertising through an account executive normally requires a minimum investment of USD $50,000, which is a different buying relationship entirely.

Can I run Amazon DSP without a Seller Central account?

Yes. DSP runs on its own advertiser seat and doesn't require you to sell on Amazon or hold a Seller Central login — it's a separate registration process from the one described in this page.
