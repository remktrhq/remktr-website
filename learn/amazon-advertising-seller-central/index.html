What "advertising in Seller Central" actually means

Seller Central is your selling account: inventory, orders, listings, payments. The Advertising tab inside it is a doorway, not a destination — clicking it drops you into the advertising console (also called Campaign Manager), which is a separate Amazon system that happens to be linked from your seller account.

You need a Professional selling plan to see that tab at all. If your account is on the Individual plan, there's no Advertising menu to click — that's the single most common reason a new seller can't find it. Upgrading the plan is the fix, not a support ticket.

Some ad types need one more thing: enrollment in Amazon Brand Registry. Without it, Sponsored Products is available but Sponsored Brands, Display, and a free Brand Store are not. Brand Registry is free and takes a trademark (or a pending application in some cases) — worth doing before you need it, not after a campaign idea stalls.

The three ad types inside the console, and what each requires

All three sit in the same console, but they're not interchangeable:

  • Sponsored Products — cost-per-click, promotes a single listing, no Brand Registry needed. This is the one every Professional seller can run on day one.
  • Sponsored Brands — CPC or vCPM, shows your logo and a headline above search results, requires Brand Registry, drives traffic to a Store or listing.
  • Sponsored Display — CPC or vCPM, runs across Amazon's own inventory and some off-Amazon placements, also requires Brand Registry, available as a simplified automated setup or a more granular one.

Amazon's own advertising page states that a fully managed option, run through an Account Executive rather than self-serve, typically requires a minimum investment of USD 50,000. Self-serve Sponsored Products has no such floor — you pay per click, starting with whatever daily budget you set.

A worked example: setting your first budget

Say a product sells for $25 and you want to hold Advertising Cost of Sale (ACOS) at 30%. Your maximum bid, roughly, is price × target ACOS × estimated conversion rate. At a 10% conversion rate, that's $25 × 0.30 × 0.10 = $0.75 as a starting max CPC — not a bid to set and forget, but a ceiling to test against.

Run that campaign for a week. If your actual ACOS comes in above 30%, either the bid is too high for how often the click converts, or the keyword is too broad and pulling in searches that were never going to buy. Lower the bid first — it's the cheaper experiment. Only cut the keyword if the bid drop doesn't move the number.

This is the whole mechanic Seller Central's console is built for: one ad, one click, one attributed sale, reported back with a lag of a day or two. It works. It's also the entire ceiling of what that view can tell you.

Where Seller Central's view runs out — and what that costs you

Everything in the Seller Central console is last-click: whichever ad the shopper touched right before buying gets the credit. That's fine when you're only running Sponsored Products. It breaks down once you add Display, or Amazon DSP, because DSP doesn't live in Seller Central at all — it runs on its own seat, usually accessed separately or through a managed service, and it reaches shoppers who haven't searched for anything yet.

When people quote Amazon DSP's cost-per-click, they usually cite a video-only figure — the $0.41 CPC that circulates in this category is an online-video number, not what a full-funnel book actually spends across formats. Across 30 advertisers we ran in July 2026, the blended book — video, display, everything — ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 CPC. Different question, different answer.

The bigger issue isn't the number — it's what last-click can't tell you: whether the display impression a shopper saw actually caused the sale, or whether they were buying anyway and the ad just happened to be the last thing they clicked. Seller Central's reports can't answer that. Holdout tests and matched controls can, and that requires reconciling DSP and sponsored data together instead of letting each system claim the same sale — which is why that same 30-advertiser book, measured that way, came back at 6.04x ROAS, a $5.49 blended cost per acquisition across 57,137 purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases from shoppers new to the brand.

Common mistakes — including ones we've made

The most common Seller Central mistake is chasing ACOS to zero. A campaign at 15% ACOS that only ever reaches shoppers already searching your brand name isn't growing anything — it's just paying for traffic you'd have gotten free. Target ACOS is a tool, not a scoreboard.

The second is enrolling in Brand Registry too late — after a competitor has already run Sponsored Brands against your listing and you can't respond in kind for weeks.

The third, and one we've made ourselves: treating early last-click DSP results as proof of incrementality before running a holdout against them. A campaign can post a strong last-click ROAS and still be spending against shoppers who were already converting — you don't find that out until you hold a matched group back and compare. We now build that comparison in before reporting a number, not after a client asks why it looks different from the console.

Which one you should actually pick

Seller Central's own console — Sponsored Products, Brands, Display — is the right starting point for any seller and plenty run it themselves indefinitely. It stops being enough once you're adding DSP or off-Amazon reach and need to know if that spend is incremental, not just attributed; that's a measurement job, not a bigger budget.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 48.5% across the 47 brands above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Do I need a Professional Seller account to advertise?

Yes, for the Seller Central-linked console. Individual plan sellers don't see the Advertising tab at all. Upgrading the plan is the only fix — there's no separate ads-only signup that bypasses it.

Why can't I see Sponsored Brands or Display in my account?

Those two, plus a free Brand Store, require enrollment in Amazon Brand Registry. Sponsored Products doesn't need it, which is why it's usually the first ad type any new seller runs.

Is Amazon DSP part of Seller Central?

No. DSP runs on its own seat and console, separate from the Seller Central advertising tab, and doesn't require a selling account at all — it can advertise brands that don't sell on Amazon. It's typically run self-serve or through a managed service, not clicked into from the seller dashboard.

How much does Amazon advertising cost?

Self-serve Sponsored Products, Brands, and Display are pay-per-click with no platform minimum — you set the budget. Amazon's own page states that its managed service, run through an Account Executive, typically requires a minimum investment of $50,000. For DSP run through a third-party managed service, check the vendor's current pricing directly; structures vary by seat fee, percentage of spend, or quote.

What if my ACOS looks fine but sales aren't growing?

That's usually a sign the campaign is only reaching people already searching for your brand — a good ACOS number with no new-customer reach. Check the new-to-brand metric in your reports before assuming the campaign is working as intended.
