What Does an "Amazon Marketing Head" Actually Do?

The short answer

"Amazon marketing head" means two different things. Inside Amazon it's a corporate title, like the Head of Marketing role at Prime Video. Inside a brand, it's whoever owns Amazon ad strategy — sponsored ads, DSP, and whether the reported ROAS is even real.

Two things people mean by "Amazon marketing head"

Search this phrase and two unrelated answers get mixed together. One is a literal job title inside Amazon corporate — a Director and Head of Marketing for a business unit like Prime Video, or an open req on Amazon's own careers page for marketing coordinators and managers. That's Amazon hiring for itself, and it has nothing to do with how your brand advertises on Amazon.

The other meaning is the one that actually matters if you sell on Amazon: the person inside your company, or your agency, responsible for how your brand shows up and gets measured on the platform. That covers sponsored ads, DSP, the brand store, and the reporting that goes to whoever signs the budget. This page is about that second version, because it's the one with a real decision behind it.

What the role should actually own inside a brand

  • Budget allocation across ad types — sponsored products, sponsored brands, sponsored display, and DSP, weighed against each other rather than funded on whichever shows the lowest CPC in isolation.
  • Catalog and brand store strategy — making sure the traffic being paid for lands somewhere that actually converts.
  • Attribution and measurement — deciding whether the business is content with last-click ROAS or wants something closer to proof that the spend caused a sale.
  • Reporting upward — turning platform dashboards into numbers a CFO will accept, including the ones that complicate the story.

At small spend, one generalist often owns all of it alongside search and social. Past a certain scale, DSP and sponsored ads pull on different skills and different tooling, and the title quietly splits into two roles — which is usually the point where brands start looking for outside help on the display side specifically.

A worked example: reading an Amazon media report properly

Numbers only tell you something once you break them apart. Here's one real block of them: across 30 advertiser accounts we ran DSP for in July 2026, the book delivered 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, a blended $1.42 CPC, 57,137 attributed purchases at a $5.49 blended CPA, and a portfolio-wide 6.04x return on ad spend. That $1.42 figure matters on its own — the $0.41 CPC often quoted in this category is an online-video number, not a whole-book number, and reporting one as the other misleads whoever's reading it.

A head of Amazon marketing who stops at the 6.04x isn't doing the job. The next line in that same report showed 20.1% of those purchases came from shoppers new to the brand — the figure that actually says whether the display spend expanded the customer base or just rode along on demand that would have converted anyway.

When the numbers are bad news

Sometimes the report says the campaign is underperforming, and the honest move is to say so rather than reframe the metric until it looks better. A few checks before declaring anything broken: is the attribution window fair to the funnel stage being measured, is spend concentrated on a handful of ASINs skewing the average, and has creative actually rotated or has the same asset been running for months.

If CPA is climbing and new-to-brand share is falling at the same time, that's usually not a media problem — it's a sign the audience being targeted has already been reached and the campaign is now paying to remind people who were going to buy anyway. The fix is changing who's targeted, not raising the bid.

The common mistake — including one we've made

The most common mistake is trusting a single blended ROAS number as if it were self-evidently correct. Sponsored ads and DSP can both take credit for the same purchase in separate reports, and a head of marketing who adds the two ROAS figures together is double-counting without realizing it. We've done exactly that on a report before catching it — which is the specific reason we now reconcile DSP and sponsored ads inside Amazon Marketing Cloud rather than reading each dashboard on its own.

The second common mistake is treating last-click attribution as proof of causation. It can tell you a purchase happened after an ad was seen. It cannot tell you the purchase wouldn't have happened anyway. Holdout tests and matched controls can answer that; last-click never could, on any platform.

Which one you should actually pick

Job seekers and anyone checking a LinkedIn title should read this as Amazon's own corporate role. Brands with modest, single-channel spend can usually keep this responsibility in-house with one generalist. Brands running DSP alongside sponsored ads at real scale need either a dedicated hire or a managed-service partner who reconciles the two rather than reporting them separately.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is "Amazon marketing head" a real job title at Amazon?

Yes — Amazon uses titles like Director and Head of Marketing inside specific business units, such as Prime Video. If you're job-hunting or checking someone's LinkedIn, that's the correct meaning. If you're trying to figure out how to run Amazon marketing for your own brand, it's a different question entirely, covered above.

Does a brand need a dedicated Amazon marketing head?

It depends on spend and complexity. A brand running a few thousand dollars a month in sponsored ads usually doesn't need a dedicated role. Once DSP, multiple ad types, and attribution questions enter the picture, someone needs to own the whole strategy rather than one channel at a time — whether that's a hire or a managed-service partner.

What's the difference between an Amazon marketing head and an Amazon marketing manager?

Seniority and scope. A manager typically executes within one channel — sponsored ads, say. A head owns budget allocation across channels, sets the measurement standard the business trusts, and reports results in terms leadership outside marketing will accept.

How do I know if my Amazon marketing head's reported ROAS is real?

Ask whether DSP and sponsored ads numbers have been reconciled against each other, or just added together. Ask whether the reported result came from last-click attribution or from a holdout or matched-control test. Last-click can show a purchase happened after an ad; it can't show the purchase wouldn't have happened without it.

What should I do if the Amazon marketing numbers are bad?

Check the attribution window, check whether spend is concentrated on a few ASINs skewing the average, and check whether creative has actually changed recently. If CPA is rising while new-to-brand share is falling, the fix is usually retargeting who's being reached, not spending more against the same audience.
