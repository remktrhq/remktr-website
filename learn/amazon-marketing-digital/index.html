The short answer

Amazon digital marketing covers three distinct things: organic listing optimization, sponsored ads (pay-per-click), and DSP (programmatic display and video, bought on impressions). Each has different economics and attribution. Most confusion — and most bad ROAS math — comes from treating them as one channel.

What people mean when they say 'Amazon digital marketing'

The phrase gets used for three different jobs, and mixing them up is where most confusion starts.

  • Organic: listing content, images, A+ content, and search rank inside Amazon's own search engine. No media spend, but no guaranteed placement either.
  • Sponsored ads: Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored TV — cost-per-click auctions that sit inside and around Amazon search results and product pages. You pay when someone clicks.
  • DSP (Demand-Side Platform): programmatic display and video bought on impressions, running across Amazon-owned properties (Fire TV, IMDb, Amazon.com) and the open web through Amazon's exchange access. You pay for reach, not clicks.

These three run on different pricing models, different placements, and — critically — different attribution windows. A dollar spent in each one earns a return that can't be compared apples-to-apples unless you know which bucket it came from.

How the stack actually fits together

Sponsored ads catch demand that already exists — someone typed a search term or is looking at a competitor's listing. DSP tries to create demand, or bring back a shopper who looked and left. Treating DSP like a bigger version of Sponsored Products is the single most common mistake in this category, and it's one we've made ourselves earlier in a program before the measurement caught up.

The practical difference: a Sponsored Products click and a DSP impression can both get credited for the same sale if you're using last-click attribution on both. Last-click doesn't know which one actually moved the shopper — it just gives the win to whichever touchpoint happened last. That's not a rounding error. On a program running both channels at scale, it can overstate one channel's contribution by a meaningful margin and make the other look like dead weight.

A worked example, with real numbers

Here's what an actual DSP program looks like once you separate the pricing model from the outcome. Across 30 advertisers in reMKTR's book in July 2026, the portfolio delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole book, not the best-performing line item. That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, with a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. Blended cost per acquisition was $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper who'd never bought the brand before.

Two things in that paragraph matter more than the headline ROAS. First, the $0.41 CPC figure that gets quoted a lot in this category is an online-video-only number — it's real, but it's not what a blended DSP program actually costs once you include display and audio. Second, the new-to-brand share (20.1%) is the number that tells you whether display is doing its actual job: reaching people sponsored ads never touch. A channel with a great ROAS but a 2% new-to-brand rate is just retargeting your existing buyers at a markup.

What to do when the number looks bad

If your DSP ROAS looks flat or negative and you're ready to kill the channel, check three things before you do:

  • Attribution model first. If DSP and Sponsored Ads are both on last-click, they're competing for credit on the same conversions. That's not a DSP problem, it's a measurement problem, and it will make display look worse than it is every single time.
  • Overlap, not addition. Amazon Marketing Cloud can show you whether a DSP impression and a Sponsored Products click touched the same shopper before the same purchase. If they did, one of those touchpoints is getting double-counted somewhere in your reporting.
  • Holdout before you cut. A raw ROAS number, high or low, doesn't tell you what would have happened without the spend. A matched-control or holdout test does. Last-click attribution cannot prove incrementality — it was never built to — so a bad ROAS on a last-click basis isn't proof the channel didn't work. It might just mean you're measuring it wrong.

If you've checked all three and the number is still bad, it's genuinely bad. Cut it. The point isn't to defend every dollar spent on display — it's to make sure the number you're killing the channel on is the real one.

Which one you should actually pick

Organic and sponsored ads are the right starting point for most sellers — cheap to test, easy to measure, and last-click attribution is defensible when demand already exists. DSP suits brands with volume and a real question about incremental reach, but only pays off if you measure it with holdouts instead of last-click. reMKTR runs 109 live DSP seats and reconciles DSP against sponsored ads in Amazon Marketing Cloud specifically because last-click can't settle whether display added anything — that's the piece worth checking before you judge any number a dashboard hands you, whoever runs your account.

Common questions

Is Amazon digital marketing the same as Amazon PPC?

No. PPC (Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands) is one piece of it — the pay-per-click auction layer. Amazon digital marketing also includes organic listing work and DSP, which is bought on impressions, not clicks. People use 'PPC' and 'Amazon marketing' interchangeably because PPC is the easiest to start with, not because it's the whole picture.

What's the actual difference between Sponsored Products and DSP?

Sponsored Products puts your listing in front of someone actively searching, and you pay per click. DSP buys display and video impressions across Amazon-owned and third-party inventory, and you pay per thousand impressions whether or not anyone clicks. One catches demand, the other tries to build or recapture it.

What CPC or CPM should I expect on Amazon?

There's no single honest number — it depends heavily on category, competition, and format. As one reference point, a blended DSP book across 30 advertisers ran a $4.00 CPM and a $1.42 blended CPC in July 2026; a commonly quoted $0.41 CPC figure floating around this category is online-video-only, not a full-funnel blend. Treat any single number you see without a format attached as incomplete.

How do I know if my display advertising is actually working?

Check the new-to-brand purchase rate before you check ROAS. If display is only reaching people who already buy from you, it's an expensive way to do what retargeting already does for less. A holdout or matched-control test is the only way to know if the spend is incremental — last-click attribution can't answer that question.

Do I need an agency to run Amazon DSP, or can I do it myself?

DSP is available self-service through Amazon Ads, and plenty of teams run it in-house. Where managed service earns its cost is in the measurement layer — reconciling DSP against sponsored ads in Amazon Marketing Cloud so the two aren't double-counting the same shoppers, and running holdouts instead of trusting last-click. If you already have that measurement discipline in-house, you may not need a managed partner for it.
