The short answer

Amazon DSP deduplicates reach through its authenticated graph — a deterministic household match built from Amazon account sign-ins, Fire TV device registrations and Prime Video streaming sessions, reaching over 90% of US households. That graph lets a campaign recognize the same household across Prime Video, Disney's bundle, Netflix and other supply sources, so gross impressions can be converted into a genuine unique-reach figure rather than an inflated sum across platforms.

The mechanism: authenticated, not modeled

Most cross-publisher reach measurement in advertising relies on probabilistic modeling — statistical inference about which impressions likely reached the same person, built from cookies, device fingerprints or panel data. Amazon's approach is different in kind, not just in accuracy. The authenticated graph is built from deterministic signals: an account sign-in, a Fire TV device registration, a Prime Video streaming session. These are direct, encrypted and pseudonymized connections to a verified household, not statistical inference, and Amazon's own published claim is over 90% of US households reached this way. Match rates on top of that base run above 75% on the open internet generally and above 80% specifically for streaming TV campaigns.

The graph also extends to Amazon's audio partnerships — Spotify, iHeart and Pandora — which matters for the streaming picture specifically because a household's identity, once authenticated, carries across screen and audio formats rather than resetting at each new device or app. For cross-publisher video specifically, the practical effect is that Amazon isn't inferring the connection between a Prime Video impression and a Hulu impression on the same TV — it's recognizing the same signed-in household or device on both, which is a materially stronger basis for both deduplication and frequency control than probabilistic matching provides.

What deduplication actually catches

Without deduplication, a household that saw an ad on Prime Video, Hulu and Netflix in the same week would count as three separate reach events in a naive sum of each platform's individual reporting — three "unique" viewers who are actually one person. The authenticated graph collapses that into a single household reached three times, which changes two numbers that matter for planning: your real unique reach (lower than the naive sum) and your real frequency (higher per household than any single platform's report would suggest on its own). Amazon's own reporting on this mechanism, applied to Interactive Video Ads specifically, cites 57% fewer redundant ad exposures as a result.

The effect compounds as a plan adds more supply sources. Two overlapping platforms produce one layer of double-counting; five overlapping platforms — Prime Video, Netflix, Disney's bundle, Fire TV and Twitch all running together — produce a naive reach sum that can overstate the real unique audience by a wide margin, which is exactly why deduplication matters more, not less, as a streaming plan grows more diversified.

A worked example

Say a campaign runs $10,000 on Prime Video and $8,000 on Hulu in the same month, each reporting roughly 350,000 and 280,000 impressions respectively — 630,000 gross impressions if you simply added the two platform reports together. With deduplication applied through the authenticated graph, a meaningful share of those households appear in both platforms' delivery, and the real unique-household reach will land below 630,000, while frequency per household will be higher than either platform's individual average suggests. The exact overlap varies by audience and targeting, but the direction of the adjustment is always the same: naive addition overstates reach and understates frequency.

What to do when the numbers don't reconcile

If a campaign's deduplicated reach report looks implausibly close to the naive sum of its individual platform reports, check whether cross-publisher frequency capping is actually enabled for that campaign — deduplication and capping both depend on the same authenticated-graph configuration, and a campaign set up with independent per-platform caps won't benefit from either. If deduplicated reach looks lower than expected relative to spend, that can be a genuine sign of audience overlap concentration — the campaign may be reaching a smaller, more frequently-hit pool of households than the budget implies, worth checking against frequency distribution rather than assuming a reporting error.

If two different reports from the same campaign show different reach figures, check the reporting window and whether one report includes third-party supply sources like Netflix or Disney's DRAX bundle and the other only covers Amazon-owned inventory — a partial-scope report and a full-scope report will legitimately disagree, and that's a scope difference, not a data-quality problem.

The common mistake

The mistake is reporting a media plan's reach as the simple sum of each platform's individually-reported unique viewers — a number that's almost always too high once real household overlap is accounted for. It's an easy mistake to make because each individual platform's report is accurate on its own terms; the error is in adding them together without deduplication. reMKTR reports cross-publisher streaming reach off Amazon's deduplicated household figures specifically because a stakeholder who's shown an inflated reach number once will discount every number reported afterward, even the accurate ones.

The second version of the mistake happens in the other direction: treating a lower deduplicated reach figure as a sign the campaign underperformed, without recognizing that the naive, undeduplicated number it's being compared against was never real in the first place. A deduplicated number that looks smaller than expected is usually a more honest number, not a worse one — the fix, if the reach genuinely needs to grow, is broader targeting or added budget, not a return to inflated platform-by-platform addition.

Which one you should actually pick

This mechanism suits any brand running streaming across more than one supply source and reporting reach to a stakeholder who will do the arithmetic themselves, or ask someone else to. A brand running a single platform doesn't need to think about deduplication at all — it only becomes relevant the moment a plan spans two or more supply sources with any meaningful audience overlap between them, which today describes most streaming plans that go beyond a single test line item.

Common questions

Does deduplication work automatically, or does it need to be configured?

Cross-publisher deduplication and frequency capping both depend on the campaign being set up to use Amazon's authenticated graph across supply sources — confirm this is enabled rather than assuming it applies by default to every campaign structure.

How accurate is Amazon's household match rate?

Amazon reports over 90% of US households reached deterministically overall, with match rates above 75% on the open internet and above 80% specifically for streaming TV campaigns, according to its own published guide to the authenticated graph.

Can deduplication work across Netflix and Disney's inventory too, not just Amazon's own properties?

The authenticated graph is designed to apply across the supply sources Amazon DSP reaches, including third-party integrations like Disney's DRAX bundle and Netflix — confirm current cross-publisher coverage in your DSP console, since integration scope can expand over time.

What's the difference between deduplication and frequency capping?

Deduplication is the measurement — knowing that two impressions on different platforms reached the same household. Frequency capping is the control — limiting how many times that household is served an ad. Both rely on the same authenticated-graph identity match, but they answer different questions.

Should I distrust a platform's own individual reach report because of this?

No — a single platform's own reach report is generally accurate for that platform in isolation. The issue only arises when reports from multiple platforms are added together without deduplication, which overstates the combined total.
