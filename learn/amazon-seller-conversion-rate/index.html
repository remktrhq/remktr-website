The short answer

Amazon seller conversion rate is the percentage of sessions on your listing that end in a purchase — found in Seller Central under Business Reports. It's units ordered divided by sessions, times 100. Most listings run 8 to 12 percent; below that usually points to a listing problem, not a traffic problem.

What Amazon Conversion Rate Actually Means

Amazon does not call it "conversion rate" inside Seller Central — it calls it Unit Session Percentage, and that's the number most sellers mean when they say conversion rate. You'll find it under Reports > Business Reports > Detail Page Sales and Traffic, broken out by ASIN, date range, and device.

The formula: units ordered ÷ sessions × 100. Sessions are deduplicated visits to your listing in a given period, on any device. Units ordered is every unit shipped as part of an order, not the number of unique buyers — a customer who buys three units still adds three to the numerator.

That distinction matters more than it looks. A listing that sells in multi-packs or bundles will show a Unit Session Percentage higher than the plain "percentage of visitors who bought," because one order can carry several units. If you're benchmarking against classic e-commerce conversion rate (orders ÷ visitors), you're comparing two different things.

A Worked Example

Say a listing gets 4,000 sessions in a week and sells 360 units. Unit Session Percentage = 360 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 9%. That sits inside the range most mid-catalog listings land in. Now say the same listing runs a coupon: sessions jump to 6,000 and units rise to 420 — the rate actually drops to 7%, even though revenue went up. Traffic grew faster than intent did.

That's the trap in reading conversion rate on its own: it's a ratio, and a ratio can fall while the business improves, or rise while it doesn't. Read it next to sessions and units, never instead of them.

What's a Good Conversion Rate — and Why Sample Size Matters More Than the Benchmark

Published ranges — roughly 8–12% average, low single digits considered weak, mid-teens considered strong — hold up reasonably well as a starting point. But they were built across categories that don't behave alike. A $12 phone case and a $400 espresso machine don't convert at the same rate, and neither does a listing with 40 reviews versus one with 4,000.

The bigger problem is sample size. A listing with 80 sessions a week and 9 orders shows 11.25% — inside the "good" band — but that figure can swing to 5% or 18% the following week on nothing but noise. Below a few hundred sessions a week, treat the weekly Unit Session Percentage as a trend line to watch, not a score to act on. Benchmarks are only meaningful once there's real volume behind the number.

The Metrics That Get Confused With Conversion Rate

Before touching a listing, know what each Business Report line actually measures. Three of these get conflated with conversion rate constantly, and the confusion sends sellers fixing the wrong thing.

Why the Number Moves — and What to Do When It's Bad News

When conversion rate drops, check in this order: Buy Box percentage first, then price and image history, then review trend, then whether the traffic mix changed — a spike in browser-heavy keywords from a new campaign can drag the rate down even if the page didn't change at all. Most sellers start with copy and images when the real cause was losing the Buy Box on 30% of sessions.

If the checks confirm the page is the actual cause: fix the main image and title first, since those decide who clicks in the first place; then check price against close competitors; then check whether recent reviews mention a specific complaint that's costing conversions. Don't change five things at once — you won't know which one moved the number.

The honest mistake we've seen agencies make, ourselves included: treating a conversion rate dip as a creative problem when it's a supply problem. If sessions are steady but units drop, check inventory and Buy Box status before rewriting a single bullet point.

Where Paid Traffic Complicates the Picture

Conversion rate also blends traffic that has no business being averaged together. A shopper who searched your brand name by name converts differently than one who saw a display ad for the first time. Across 30 of the advertiser accounts we manage, in July 2026, that book ran a blended cost-per-acquisition of $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases — and 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand. New-to-brand traffic and repeat-intent traffic don't convert at the same rate, and folding them into one Unit Session Percentage on the listing hides which kind of demand you're actually building.

This is also where last-click attribution misleads people: it credits whichever ad ran last before the sale, not whichever ad actually caused it. The only way to know if upper-funnel traffic — display, video — is adding buyers rather than just touching shoppers who were converting anyway is a holdout test or a matched-control study, not a dashboard.

Which one you should actually pick

Sellers with low weekly sessions should watch the trend, not chase the benchmark — noise will fool you at that volume. Listings with real traffic should benchmark against category peers and rule out a Buy Box problem before touching creative. Brands already running DSP or Sponsored Display need incrementality testing, not last-click, to know if upper-funnel traffic is converting anyone new.

Common questions

What is a good Amazon seller conversion rate?

Roughly 8–12% is average for most categories, with low single digits considered weak and mid-teens considered strong. But these ranges assume real session volume behind them — a listing getting under a few hundred sessions a week will bounce around these bands on noise alone, so treat the number as a trend, not a score, until volume is there.

Where do I check conversion rate in Amazon Seller Central?

Go to Reports, then Business Reports, then Detail Page Sales and Traffic. The column labeled Unit Session Percentage is what most people mean by conversion rate. You can break it out by ASIN, date range, and device from that same report.

Why did my Amazon conversion rate drop suddenly?

Check Buy Box percentage first — losing it on even a third of sessions will drag the rate down with nothing else changing. After that, check for a price increase, a stockout, a new negative review theme, or a shift in traffic mix from a campaign sending in more browsers than buyers.

Does Amazon's conversion rate include advertising clicks?

Yes — Unit Session Percentage in Business Reports counts every session regardless of whether it came from an ad, organic search, or a direct link. Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands reporting also shows its own conversion figure, but that one is scoped to ad-attributed sales only, which is a narrower number than the listing-wide session rate.

Can an Amazon conversion rate be too high?

It can signal two different things: either you're under-investing in advertising relative to demand, or your session count is low enough that the percentage is statistical noise rather than a real signal. Check sessions before assuming a high number means you should pull back on ads.
