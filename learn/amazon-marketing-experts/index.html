What "Amazon marketing expert" actually means

"Amazon marketing expert" isn't a licensed title. Anyone can put it on a Fiverr profile or a LinkedIn header. The label covers three genuinely different jobs, and most people hiring one don't realize which job they actually need.

  • Listing and SEO — product titles, bullets, backend keywords, A+ content. This is copywriting plus Amazon's search algorithm.
  • Sponsored ads (PPC) — Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display, bid management inside Seller Central or the Ad Console.
  • DSP and programmatic display — off- and on-Amazon display and retargeting. This requires an actual DSP seat and reports at a different layer entirely.

A freelancer who's excellent at listing copy may have never touched a DSP console. An agency that runs strong PPC may resell DSP to a third party and mark it up. Ask which of the three you're actually hiring for before you look at a single portfolio.

What good numbers actually look like

Here's a real portfolio result, so you have something concrete to compare an expert's pitch against. Across 30 advertisers we ran through Amazon DSP in July 2026, the portfolio returned 6.04x ROAS — measured across the whole book, not the single best line item pulled out to look good in a deck. That book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, with a blended cost per acquisition of $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases. 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand.

Two things worth flagging in that number. First, the $0.41 CPC that gets quoted around this category is online-video only, not the whole book — if a pitch deck leads with a rock-bottom CPC, ask which format it's measuring. Second, "attributed purchases" is not the same as "purchases that wouldn't have happened anyway." Last-click attribution tells you a sale happened near an ad. It can't tell you the ad caused it. That takes holdouts or matched controls, and most experts — including agencies charging for the report — don't run either.

How to vet one before you sign anything

Ask these five questions. The answers tell you more than a star rating ever will.

  • Which of the three layers — listing, PPC, DSP — do you actually operate, and which do you subcontract?
  • Can you show a result across a whole account or portfolio, not one cherry-picked campaign?
  • How do you separate what DSP added from what sponsored ads would have driven anyway?
  • What's your fee structure — flat, per-seat, banded by spend, or a percentage — and what is the percentage?
  • If we leave, do we keep the account structure and the underlying data, or does reporting access disappear with you?

If the answer to the fee question is "we'll send a custom quote," that's normal — most legitimate operators price this way once they know your spend. What's not normal is refusing to name the structure at all. A vendor who won't say whether they charge a flat fee or a percentage of your spend is a vendor who doesn't want you doing the math on what a growing budget will cost you next year.

The mistake almost everyone makes here

The single most common mistake in hiring an Amazon marketing expert is judging them on last-click ROAS alone — and we've made a version of this mistake ourselves early on, reporting DSP performance without reconciling it against sponsored ads running in parallel. When DSP and Sponsored Display both get credit for the same sale, the reported return looks better than the real one, and nobody catches it until the budget doubles and the incremental lift doesn't show up.

If a number your expert reports seems too good for your category, ask how it's measured. If they can't answer without the phrase "last click," ask for a holdout test or a matched-control period before you approve the next budget increase. If they can't run one, that's useful information too — it tells you the ceiling of what that relationship can actually prove.

Which one you should actually pick

A one-off listing fix or PPC audit suits a Fiverr freelancer. A hands-on operator for a growing brand suits a vetted independent consultant. A brand wanting SEO, content and ads under one roof suits a full-service agency, accepting bundled reporting as the trade-off. What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What does an Amazon marketing expert actually do?

It depends entirely on which layer they work in. Some do listing copy and SEO, some run sponsored ads bidding, and a smaller group operates DSP seats for display and retargeting. The title itself doesn't tell you which — ask directly before you hire.

How much does an Amazon marketing expert cost?

The structure varies more than any single number: flat per-project fees on freelance marketplaces, monthly retainers for independent consultants, and custom quotes — often a retainer plus a percentage of spend — for full-service agencies and DSP specialists. Check a vendor's current pricing directly, and always ask for the percentage, not just the base fee.

What's the difference between an Amazon marketing expert and an Amazon advertising agency?

An "expert" is usually one person — a freelancer or independent consultant — working solo or with a small support team. An agency bundles multiple disciplines (SEO, content, PPC, sometimes DSP) under one contract and one account manager. Agencies can be more convenient; they can also make it harder to see which channel actually drove a given sale.

Do I need a DSP expert or just a PPC expert?

If you're not yet spending consistently on sponsored ads, start with PPC — DSP's incremental lift is hard to measure and not worth the setup at small budgets. Once sponsored ads are established and you're wondering whether display would add anything on top, that's when a DSP specialist earns their fee, provided they can measure the answer with something better than last-click.

How do I know if my current Amazon expert is any good?

Ask for a result across the whole account, not one campaign. Ask how they attribute a sale to an ad — if the answer stops at last-click, push for a holdout or matched-control test before increasing budget. Weak operators can usually produce a good-looking screenshot; they struggle to produce a portfolio-wide number or explain how it was measured.
