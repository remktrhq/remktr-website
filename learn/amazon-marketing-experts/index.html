The short answer

An Amazon marketing expert is a freelancer, consultant or agency running one of four jobs on your account: listing and SEO, sponsored ads (PPC), branding and creative, or Amazon DSP display. The title is unlicensed, so vet by which layer they actually operate and whether they can prove results beyond last-click.

What "Amazon marketing experts" actually means

"Amazon marketing expert" is not a licensed title. Anyone can put it on a marketplace profile or a LinkedIn headline, which is why the phrase pulls up everything from a $10 listing rewrite to a managed display team running six-figure budgets. The label is doing no work. The layer underneath it is doing all of it.

People searching for Amazon marketing services experts, Amazon marketplace experts, Amazon PPC expert services and Amazon marketing specialists are usually looking for the same thing and describing it four different ways. In practice the work splits into four jobs:

  • Listing and SEO — titles, bullets, backend keywords, A+ content, indexing. Copywriting plus Amazon's search algorithm.
  • Sponsored ads (PPC) — Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display: bids, negatives, search term reports, budget pacing inside Seller Central or the Ads Console.
  • Branding and creative — brand story, imagery, video, storefront, the assets that lift conversion once traffic arrives.
  • Amazon DSP and programmatic display — on-Amazon and off-Amazon display, video and streaming TV, bought on impressions rather than clicks.

Someone excellent at listing copy may never have opened a DSP console. An agency strong on PPC may buy display through a third party. Ask which of the four you are actually hiring for before you look at a single case study — most of the disappointment in this category comes from buying one job and expecting another.

The four jobs, and which one you actually need

Listing and SEO experts are the right first hire when your product pages do not convert or your keywords are not indexed. They are cheap relative to the alternatives and the work is finite: once the listing is right, it stays mostly right. If your ads get clicks and no sales, this is the problem, and no bid adjustment will fix it.

PPC experts matter once you have spend worth managing. The job is unglamorous and measurable: pull the search term report, negate what is spending without converting, shift budget to what is, and keep doing it weekly. A good PPC operator will tell you within a month which of your keywords are subsidising the rest.

Branding and creative specialists earn their fee when you have traffic and a conversion problem, or when you are launching and have no brand assets at all. They are the hardest of the four to judge on numbers, because the effect shows up in conversion rate rather than in a campaign dashboard.

DSP specialists are the last hire, not the first. Display and streaming reach people who have not searched for you yet, which means the measurement question changes completely — and a specialist who cannot answer that question is not a specialist. More on that below.

The tiers, and how each one actually prices

Price structure tells you more than the headline number, and the structures here are genuinely different rather than just bigger and smaller versions of each other.

  • Freelancers on marketplaces — flat per-project fees, scoped tightly. Good for a single defined fix: one listing rewrite, one backend keyword cleanup, one PPC audit. The trade-off is continuity; a project ends when the deliverable ships.
  • Independent consultants and vetted marketplace picks — a monthly retainer, sometimes with a performance component. Vetting platforms typically band experts by the client budget size they handle, which is a useful signal in itself. Good for a brand that wants one accountable operator rather than a rotating team.
  • Full-service agencies — a custom quote, commonly a retainer plus a percentage of ad spend. Good when you want SEO, content, PPC and creative under one contract and one account manager. The trade-off is bundled reporting, which can make it harder to see which channel drove a given sale.
  • Managed DSP specialists — a fee or a percentage of spend, and the meaningful split is whether they hold their own DSP accounts or resell someone else's access. Ask directly; it changes who owns the data.
  • Software instead of a person — for PPC specifically, an autonomous tool can cover the weekly optimisation loop at a fraction of a retainer. Dr. PPC is $300 a month plus 2% of ad spend, the first 30 days are $1, Orbit is included and there is no contract — worth pricing against a retainer before you assume you need a human for the routine work.

If a vendor will not say whether they charge a flat fee or a percentage of your spend, that is the finding. Not because a percentage is wrong — plenty of good operators charge that way — but because you cannot forecast next year's cost without it.

What good numbers actually look like

You need a benchmark to compare a pitch against, so here is one from our own book, scoped precisely. Across 30 advertisers we ran through Amazon DSP in July 2026, the portfolio returned 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole book, not the best line item pulled out for a deck. That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, with a blended cost per acquisition of $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases. 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand.

Two things to notice. First, the $0.41 CPC that circulates in this category is an online-video-only figure, not a whole-book number — if a pitch leads with a rock-bottom CPC, ask which placement it came from. Second, "attributed purchases" is not the same as "purchases that would not have happened otherwise." Last-click tells you a sale happened near an ad. It cannot tell you the ad caused it.

Five questions to ask before you sign anything

These are the questions that separate an operator from a reseller. The answers matter more than any star rating.

  • Which of the four layers do you operate directly, and which do you subcontract? A straight answer here is a good sign even when the answer is "we subcontract display."
  • Can you show a result across a whole account or portfolio, not one campaign? Anyone can screenshot a good week. Portfolio-wide numbers are harder to fake and harder to produce.
  • How do you separate what display added from what sponsored ads would have driven anyway? If the answer stops at last-click, you have found the ceiling of what that relationship can prove.
  • What is the fee structure, and what is the percentage? Flat, per-seat, banded by spend, or a share of spend — all legitimate, all different as your budget grows. One hidden point on $100,000 a month is $12,000 a year.
  • If we leave, do we keep the account structure and the data? Ask before you sign, because afterwards the answer is whatever they decide it is.

"We will send a custom quote" is a normal answer to the fee question once they know your spend. Refusing to name the structure at all is not.

The mistake almost everyone makes here

The most common mistake in hiring an Amazon marketing expert is judging them on last-click ROAS alone — and we have made a version of it ourselves, reporting display performance without reconciling it against sponsored ads running in parallel. When DSP and Sponsored Display both get credit for the same sale, the reported return looks better than the real one, and nobody notices until the budget doubles and the incremental lift does not arrive.

The second mistake is buying the wrong layer. A brand with a conversion problem hires a PPC expert, the PPC expert improves click efficiency, and sales stay flat because the listing was the constraint all along. Before you hire, work out which of the four jobs is actually failing: no impressions is a targeting problem, impressions without clicks is creative or price, clicks without sales is the listing, and sales without profit is a margin and fee problem that no marketer can fix.

Which one you should actually pick

A single listing rewrite or one-off PPC audit suits a marketplace freelancer. A growing brand wanting one accountable operator suits an independent consultant. A brand wanting SEO, content, PPC and creative under one contract suits a full-service agency, accepting bundled reporting as the trade-off. A brand already spending seriously on sponsored ads, wanting proof that display adds anything, suits a managed DSP specialist. And if the job is just the weekly PPC loop, software covers it for less.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 48.5% across the 47 brands above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What does an Amazon marketing expert actually do?

It depends which layer they work in. Some write listings and handle SEO, some run sponsored ads bidding, some produce brand and creative assets, and a smaller group operates Amazon DSP for display and streaming. The title does not tell you which — ask directly before you hire, and ask which parts they subcontract.

How much do Amazon marketing experts cost?

The structure varies more than any single figure: flat per-project fees on freelance marketplaces, monthly retainers for independent consultants, and custom quotes — often a retainer plus a percentage of ad spend — for agencies and DSP specialists. Ask for the percentage, not just the base fee, because that is the part that grows with your budget. For the PPC loop specifically, software is a real alternative: Dr. PPC is $300 a month plus 2% of ad spend, first 30 days $1, no contract.

What's the difference between an Amazon marketing expert and an Amazon advertising agency?

An expert is usually one person — a freelancer or independent consultant — working solo or with a small support team. An agency bundles several disciplines under one contract and one account manager. Agencies are more convenient and cover more ground; the trade-off is that bundled reporting can make it harder to see which channel actually drove a sale.

Do I need a DSP expert or just a PPC expert?

Start with PPC. If you are not yet spending consistently on sponsored ads, display's incremental lift is hard to measure and hard to justify. Once sponsored ads are established and you are asking whether display would add anything on top, a DSP specialist earns their fee — provided they can answer that question with something better than last-click.

How do I know if my current Amazon expert is any good?

Ask for a result across the whole account, not one campaign. Ask how they attribute a sale to an ad — if the answer stops at last-click, push for a holdout or matched-control period before you increase budget. Weak operators can usually produce a good-looking screenshot; they struggle to produce a portfolio-wide number or explain how it was measured.
