Amazon FBA Conversion Rate: What It Is, What's Good, and What to Do When It Looks Wrong

The short answer

Amazon conversion rate — Amazon's own dashboard calls it Unit Session Percentage — is units ordered divided by sessions, times 100. There is no single 'good' number. Category, price point, review count, and which traffic source you're measuring all shift the benchmark by multiples, not by a point or two.

What the number actually measures

Log into Seller Central, go to Business Reports, then Detail Page Sales and Traffic, and you'll find it labeled Unit Session Percentage — not "conversion rate." That naming gap is why sellers cross-reference three tools and get three different figures. Unit Session Percentage is units ordered divided by sessions. Session Percentage is orders divided by sessions. They diverge whenever someone buys more than one unit in a visit, which is why Unit Session Percentage can technically read above 100%.

Worked example: a listing gets 5,000 sessions in a week and sells 400 units. 400 ÷ 5,000 = 0.08, times 100 = 8% Unit Session Percentage. That's the number in front of you. It tells you nothing yet about whether 8% is good — that depends on what you're selling and who's landing on the page.

What's good — and why the benchmarks you'll find disagree

You'll see two contradictory claims online. One says Amazon conversion rates run 8–12% as "average" because Amazon shoppers already have purchase intent. Another cites general ecommerce benchmarks of 2–4%. Both are true — they're measuring different things. The 2–4% figures describe open-web stores where visitors are still deciding whether to buy anything at all. Amazon sessions are mostly people who already searched a specific product, so the floor is naturally higher. Comparing an Amazon listing's rate against a generic ecommerce benchmark is the single most common misread in this category.

Within Amazon itself, the range is still wide. A low-price consumable with strong review volume and repeat purchase behavior can run well into double digits. A considered, higher-price purchase with a longer decision cycle can convert respectably in the mid-single digits and be perfectly healthy. Price point, category norm, review count, and image/A+ quality all move the number independently of each other — there isn't one line you're either above or below.

Diagnose before you optimize

A low rate has two different root causes and they need opposite fixes:

  • Traffic problem: the sessions arriving aren't the right shoppers — broad or irrelevant keywords, low-intent placements, or clicks from people who were never going to buy this item at this price.
  • Listing problem: the right shoppers are arriving and leaving anyway — weak main image, thin review count, price out of line with comparable listings, missing A+ content answering the objection that's stopping the click-to-buy.

The fastest way to tell them apart is to stop reading the blended number and split it by traffic source. A listing with a healthy organic conversion rate and a weak PPC conversion rate has a targeting problem in the campaigns, not a listing problem. Fix the wrong half and you'll spend weeks tuning images that were never the issue.

The mistake that hides in blended numbers

This is where a single conversion-rate figure actively misleads once you're running more than search ads. Display and DSP traffic is deliberately upper-funnel — it's built to introduce the product, not close it on the first click. Judged by last-click session percentage alone, that traffic often looks like it converted at close to zero, and a lot of sellers pull the plug on it for that reason. Last-click attribution was never built to prove whether that exposure changed anything downstream — only a holdout or matched control can do that.

Across 30 of reMKTR's advertisers in July 2026, the portfolio delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole book, not cherry-picked from the best line item. That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, with a $5.49 blended cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases — and 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand. None of that shows up in a listing's Unit Session Percentage. It shows up when you reconcile DSP and sponsored ads together, in Amazon Marketing Cloud, so the two channels stop double-counting the same shopper.

When the fix doesn't work

Sometimes you correct the obvious problem and the rate doesn't move. Before assuming the fix failed, rule out the boring explanations: a stockout during the measurement window (sessions still counted, no purchases possible), an image or A+ update that hasn't finished indexing across the catalog, a competitor price drop that happened the same week, or simple seasonality dragging the whole category down at once. Pull the date range apart by week before concluding the change didn't work — a fix that looks flat over 30 days sometimes shows a clear break the day it went live, buried by a bad prior week.

If the rate is unusually high — Amazon's own inventory can sell through fast enough that a 16%+ reading means you're under-advertising relative to demand, not that something's broken. That's a scaling decision, not a conversion problem.

Common questions

What is the average Amazon FBA conversion rate?

There isn't one universal average worth repeating — it varies by category and price point by multiples, not by a percentage point or two. Treat any single quoted "average" as a starting benchmark for your specific category, not a target.

Is a 20% conversion rate good on Amazon?

For a low-price, well-reviewed consumable, 20% can be normal. For a considered, higher-price purchase, a rate that high can actually mean you're leaving demand on the table by under-advertising, since the listing is converting nearly everyone who reaches it.

Why did my conversion rate drop with no listing changes?

Check for a stockout in the window, a competitor price move, seasonality in the category, or a shift in your PPC targeting that changed who's landing on the page. The listing not changing doesn't mean nothing changed around it.

Does FBA versus FBM affect conversion rate?

FBA listings often convert better because of Prime eligibility and faster shipping promises, which reduce checkout hesitation. It's a real factor, but it sits alongside price, reviews, and images — not a substitute for fixing those.

Where do I find my actual conversion rate in Seller Central?

Business Reports > Detail Page Sales and Traffic, under the column labeled Unit Session Percentage. That's the figure most tools and articles are referring to when they say "conversion rate," even though Amazon doesn't use that term itself.
