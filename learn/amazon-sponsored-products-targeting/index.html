The short answer

Sponsored Products targeting is how you tell Amazon which searches and product pages should trigger your ad. There are three types — automatic, manual keyword, and manual product — plus negative targeting to exclude waste. Most accounts run automatic first to find terms, then move winners into manual.

What Targeting Actually Controls

Targeting is the setting that decides which searches and product pages your Sponsored Products ad is allowed to appear against. It's separate from bidding — targeting decides eligibility, bidding decides how hard you compete for that eligible placement. Get targeting wrong and no bid fixes it, because you're paying to be relevant to the wrong shopper in the first place.

Amazon gives you three targeting types inside Sponsored Products: automatic, manual, and negative. Manual splits again into keyword targeting and product targeting. None of these are mutually exclusive at the account level — most mature accounts run all of them at once, just not inside the same campaign, because Amazon locks the targeting type once a campaign goes live.

The Three Targeting Types, Side by Side

Here's what each one actually does, who's driving it, and where it earns its keep.

How Automatic Targeting's Four Strategies Split Your Budget

Automatic targeting runs on four strategies, and you can bid on each one separately instead of accepting one blended bid. Say HexClad is running automatic targeting on a cast iron skillet listing:

  • Close match — shoppers searching close to the listing itself, e.g. "cast iron skillet."
  • Loose match — broader, related searches, e.g. "cookware set."
  • Substitutes — product pages for other skillets, cast iron or not.
  • Complements — product pages for things bought alongside a skillet, like trivets or oven mitts.

Close match usually converts best because intent is highest. Complements usually gets the most impressions for the least conversion, because a shopper looking at oven mitts isn't necessarily buying a skillet. The most common early mistake is leaving all four on one default bid: complements and loose match soak up budget with a fine-looking CTR and a weak conversion rate, and the campaign looks healthy in aggregate while quietly wasting spend one strategy at a time. Split the bids, watch complements on its own, and cut it if it hasn't earned its place after a few weeks of data.

Manual Targeting: Keywords vs Products, and the Match Types That Change Everything

Manual keyword targeting is you choosing the exact search terms, with three match types controlling how loosely a shopper's search has to resemble your keyword before it triggers your ad:

  • Broad match — matches searches containing your keyword's terms in any order, plus close variations.
  • Phrase match — matches searches containing your exact phrase, with words allowed before or after.
  • Exact match — matches only the search term itself, or very close variants.

Manual product targeting skips keywords entirely and lets you pick specific ASINs or whole categories, refined by price band, star rating, and Prime eligibility. Choose keyword targeting when you already know how shoppers search for what you sell. Choose product targeting when you want to appear on a specific competitor's page, defend your own listing from being targeted, or sell an accessory against the product it's used with.

Negative Targeting and the Auto-to-Manual Workflow

Negative targeting doesn't generate impressions — it removes them. Add a search term or ASIN as negative and your ad stops being eligible to show against it, in either an automatic or a manual campaign.

The workflow that actually works: run automatic targeting to find out what shoppers search, pull the search term report once enough data accumulates, move the terms that convert into a manual exact-match campaign where you control the bid precisely, then add those same terms as negative exact inside the automatic campaign. Skip that last step and you're bidding against yourself for the same shopper in two campaigns at once — we've done this on our own accounts, watched CPCs creep up for no visible reason, and traced it back to an automatic campaign that never got the negative added after harvesting.

When Your Targeting Data Says Something's Wrong

Targeting problems show up as one of three symptoms, and each has a different fix:

  • ACOS is climbing on automatic targeting. Check which of the four strategies is spending — usually loose match or complements. Lower that specific bid or negative out the worst offenders before touching the campaign as a whole.
  • You harvested a converting term into manual and it stopped converting. This usually isn't a targeting problem. Check the listing itself — price, stock, star rating — before assuming the targeting setup is broken.
  • You added negatives and spend didn't drop. Confirm you added the negative at the right match type and in the right campaign; a negative phrase match added when the waste was coming in on broad match won't block it.

And the one setting people forget: once a campaign is live, you cannot switch it between automatic and manual. If you picked wrong, the fix is a new campaign, not a settings change.

A Note on Benchmarks

One habit worth breaking: treating a single average as the whole picture. It happens inside Sponsored Products when someone quotes "average ACOS" for a category without saying which match type or strategy it came from, and it happens across channels too. In our own DSP book, one CPM and CPC figure gets quoted constantly — but that same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, while the $0.41 CPC people quote in this category is online-video only, not the whole book. A number without its source line isn't a number you can act on.

Which one you should actually pick

Automatic targeting suits new listings and ongoing keyword discovery; manual targeting suits proven terms and defending or attacking specific pages; negative targeting suits cleaning up either. Handle it yourself if you have hours to check search term reports weekly. If Sponsored Products sits alongside DSP, the harder question — did display incrementally add sales — needs AMC-level measurement, not more targeting tweaks.

Common questions

What's the difference between automatic and manual targeting in Sponsored Products?

Automatic targeting lets Amazon match your ad to relevant searches and product pages using four built-in strategies. Manual targeting means you choose the exact keywords or ASINs yourself, with more control over spend but no help finding new terms. Most accounts use automatic to discover, then manual to scale what works.

Can I switch a campaign from automatic to manual targeting later?

No. Amazon locks the targeting type once a campaign is published. If you want to change strategy, you build a new campaign and move budget to it rather than editing the existing one.

How do I know which negative keywords to add?

Pull the search term report on your automatic campaign and look for terms that generated clicks but no sales, or terms that are simply irrelevant to what you sell. Add the irrelevant ones as negative exact match so they stop drawing spend; leave borderline terms running a bit longer before deciding.

Should I use keyword targeting or product targeting?

Use keyword targeting when you know how shoppers search for your product and want to compete on those terms. Use product targeting when you want to appear on a specific product's page — a competitor's listing, a complementary item, or your own listing to defend it from being targeted by others.

Why did my ACOS get worse after I turned on automatic targeting?

Usually one of the four automatic strategies — most often loose match or complements — is spending more than it's converting while close match performs fine. Check per-strategy performance before assuming automatic targeting itself is the problem; it's more often one slice of it.
