What Amazon actually owns

The NFL's long-term media distribution agreements, completed in 2021, gave Amazon exclusive rights to Thursday Night Football for an 11-year term running from the 2023 season through 2033 — the first time a streaming service, rather than a broadcast or cable network, held a full national NFL package outright. The reported value is roughly $11 billion over the term, close to $1 billion annually. That exclusivity is the whole reason this inventory behaves differently from every other streaming environment: there is no alternative broadcast a Thursday-night viewer can switch to for that specific game, which is not true of nearly anything else in this product's inventory.

Prime Video is the primary destination for the games, and TNF ad inventory is sold both through Amazon's own streaming TV product directly and through Amazon DSP, which lets a brand fold TNF into a broader streaming media plan rather than buying it as a standalone sponsorship.

Amazon-owned inventory this rights deal touches also extends beyond the single Thursday broadcast — Fire TV, Twitch and Amazon's own streaming TV product all sit under the same roof, so a plan built around a TNF week can reuse the same creative and the same authenticated-graph targeting on Amazon's other owned surfaces without starting from scratch.

What it costs, in structure

Amazon has not published a fixed rate card for TNF inventory specifically, and any number you see quoted for a single game should be read as a snapshot rather than a standing price. What is publicly reported, and useful as a planning anchor, is that premium content adjacency generally — inventory sitting next to genuinely appointment viewing rather than filler — runs in the roughly $40–$65 CPM range in the industry, well above the $20–$50 range quoted for premium streaming broadly and far above the $15–$30 range for ad-supported catch-all tiers. TNF, as a live national NFL broadcast, sits at or above the top of that premium band. Treat any lower figure you're quoted with suspicion until you've confirmed it is genuinely TNF inventory and not a cheaper adjacent placement being sold under the same umbrella.

What buys into that premium band, structurally, is exclusivity and concentration: there is exactly one Thursday-night national NFL game each week, no competing broadcast, and a fixed three-hour window in which nearly the entire available audience is watching at once. Almost nothing else in a streaming media plan compresses that much attention into that little time, which is the actual mechanism behind the premium, not just a label attached to sports content generally.

Who it actually reaches

TNF is a single weekly national broadcast during the NFL season, which means the reach is concentrated in a way almost nothing else in a streaming plan is. Amazon's own DSP inventory around TNF also carries the authenticated-graph targeting that runs across the rest of its streaming supply — built from account sign-ins, Fire TV device registrations and Prime Video streaming sessions, reaching over 90% of US households deterministically — so a brand can layer its own audience targeting on top of the live broadcast rather than buying pure run-of-game inventory blind.

A worked example

Say you commit $25,000 to a single TNF week as a test. At a representative $50 CPM — mid-range for premium live-sports content adjacency — that buys 500,000 impressions concentrated inside a three-hour national window, a scale of concentrated attention that a month of evenly-paced on-demand streaming at the same budget could not replicate. Set expectations accordingly: across 27 advertisers in our own Amazon DSP book over 31 days this summer, connected-TV and streaming inventory overall returned 0.77x on strict last-click attribution and carried the highest new-to-brand rate on the report, 56.3% — a single national live event is reach and awareness inventory first, and the honest read of a $25,000 TNF test is completion rate and branded search movement in the days after, not a same-week sales attribution number.

What to do when it goes wrong

If cost per impression on a TNF week is sharply higher than your regular streaming CPM, that is close to the point of the format, not a sign something broke — every advertiser who wants national live-sports reach is bidding into the same finite inventory at once, the same demand-spike dynamic that roughly doubles DSP click costs across the whole platform during Amazon's own Prime Week. If a single TNF test doesn't move sales in the following week, don't conclude the format failed before checking branded search and Sponsored Products engagement in Amazon Marketing Cloud — a single live event is more likely to move the top of the funnel than to close a sale by itself.

The common mistake

The mistake is buying a single TNF week as a one-off test and expecting it to prove itself the way a two-week sponsored-ads test would. Live-event reach compounds with repetition and with the rest of a brand's streaming presence — a household that saw a TNF spot and then sees the same brand again on Prime Video the following week is a different case than a household that saw it once and never again. reMKTR treats a TNF buy as one recurring line in a season-long plan rather than a single-game bet, and reads it against branded search and completion rate rather than against a click-based scoreboard the format was never built to move.

Which one you should actually pick

TNF suits a brand with the budget to treat one national weekly window as a recurring reach line, especially one that can pair it with retargeting elsewhere in the funnel to catch the demand it creates. A brand testing streaming for the first time on a small budget is better served starting with lower-cost, more flexible on-demand inventory and adding a TNF week once it has a working measurement plan in place.

Common questions

Is Thursday Night Football only available through Amazon's own ad products, or also through Amazon DSP?

Both. TNF ad inventory sells through Amazon's own streaming TV product directly and through Amazon DSP, which lets it sit inside a broader programmatic streaming plan rather than requiring a standalone sponsorship deal.

How long does Amazon hold the rights to Thursday Night Football?

Through the 2033 NFL season, under an 11-year agreement that began with the 2023 season, as part of the NFL's broader long-term media distribution agreements completed in 2021.

Does Amazon publish a fixed price for TNF ad inventory?

No. There is no public rate card for TNF specifically. Any CPM quoted for it should be treated as auction-driven and current only for the moment it was quoted, and premium live-sports content generally trades well above standard streaming CPM in industry-reported benchmarks.

Can I target specific audiences within the TNF broadcast, or is it run-of-game only?

Amazon's authenticated graph applies to TNF inventory the same way it applies to the rest of Amazon's streaming supply, so household-level and audience-based targeting is generally available rather than pure run-of-game placement — confirm current targeting options in your DSP console.

How many TNF games are included in Amazon's package each season?

At the time the rights deal was announced, the package covered 15 regular-season games annually plus one preseason game and one Wild Card playoff game — check the current season's confirmed schedule directly, since exact game counts and matchups are set by the NFL each year.

What budget do I need before a TNF buy is worth doing?

There is no published minimum specific to TNF, but given the premium CPM band this inventory trades in, a budget too small to buy a genuinely visible share of a single game's impressions is better spent building reach elsewhere in the streaming plan first.
