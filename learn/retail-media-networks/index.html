What a Retail Media Network Actually Is

A retail media network (RMN) is the advertising arm of a retailer. The retailer owns two things advertisers want: shoppers who are close to buying, and a record of what those shoppers actually bought. It sells access to both. A brand pays to appear in search results or on product pages, or to reach known shoppers elsewhere, and the retailer reports back using its own sales data.

That is the difference from most digital advertising. A social or search platform can tell you someone clicked. A retailer can often tell you whether that person later bought, because the checkout happens on the retailer's own system. This is called closed-loop measurement, and it is the main reason retail media has grown into a core line in many brands' media plans.

Almost every large retailer now runs some version of this, and so do many smaller ones. Amazon Ads is the biggest network for most online brands, which is why the rest of this page uses it as the working example.

The Three Places Retail Media Ads Show Up

Retail media networks sell a few different kinds of inventory. Knowing which is which makes every network's media kit easier to read.

  • On-site ads. Sponsored listings in search results and on product pages, usually bought per click in an auction. This is closest to the sale and the easiest to start. On Amazon these are Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display.
  • Off-site ads. The retailer's shopper data is used to show ads on other websites, apps and streaming services. These are usually bought per thousand impressions. On Amazon this is Amazon DSP, which reMKTR runs as a service.
  • In-store and owned-media ads. Screens, receipts, email and app placements. These matter most for retailers with physical stores.

Most brands should start on-site, where intent is highest, and add off-site once they want reach beyond people already searching.

How Amazon Fits In, With Real Numbers

Amazon's network is split into the two systems above: self-serve sponsored ads billed per click, and Amazon DSP billed per impression. Many brands only ever use the first. The second is where reach and measurement questions get harder.

For a sense of what off-site retail media looks like in practice: across 30 advertisers we manage on Amazon DSP in July 2026, the portfolio ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost per click. It produced 57,137 attributed purchases at a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition, with a 6.04x return on ad spend across the whole book. 20.1% of those purchases came from shoppers new to the brand.

That is one mixed portfolio in one month, not a promise for your account. It is useful as a sanity check on the scale of costs and results, and as a reminder to ask any vendor which window and how many advertisers sit behind a number they quote.

What Most Explainers Leave Out: Measurement Is Not Neutral

Every network reports its own results on its own system. That is useful, and it is also a reason to ask questions. Three are worth putting to any retail media network, or to anyone who manages it for you.

  • What counts as an attributed sale? Ask for the attribution window and whether the sale would have happened without the ad.
  • Who designs and computes any incrementality test? A holdout test is the cleanest answer to whether an ad added sales. It matters whether the party being measured designs it, and whether it is written into the agreement.
  • Are on-site and off-site results reconciled? A shopper can see a display ad and then click a sponsored listing. Without reconciling the two, the same purchase can be claimed twice. Inside Amazon, Amazon Marketing Cloud is where that reconciliation is done.

These questions apply to every vendor, including us.

A Checklist for Judging Any Retail Media Network

Before you put budget into a network, run through this list:

  • Does the retailer's audience match where your customers actually shop?
  • Is the minimum budget realistic for your size? Check the network's own current page, since minimums change.
  • Can you buy on-site and off-site, or only one?
  • Do you get search term, placement or line-item level reports, or only a summary?
  • Is there a way to measure new-to-brand shoppers?
  • What are the data rules, and can you take your results with you if you leave?
  • How does the retailer or agency charge: flat fee, percentage of ad spend, or a mix? Compare on the same basis.

Smaller brands can often get most of the value from a single network done well, instead of spreading thin across many.

Who Should Use Which Route

If you sell on Amazon and are new to advertising, start with sponsored ads on your own catalog. They need no minimum and give you your first real data. Which one you should actually pick

Retail media networks suit brands that want to advertise where shoppers already buy and see results tied to sales. Start on-site with sponsored ads; add off-site once you want reach and can measure it properly. For Amazon DSP run as a service, reMKTR is built for that. Other networks may fit better if your customers shop elsewhere.

What to do with this

Whoever runs your DSP, a partner or your own team, ask them two things: how much of DSP's attributed sales also had a sponsored ads click, and how they would hold out part of the audience in month one. Back whoever answers with a method rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What is a retail media network in simple terms?

It is a retailer's advertising business. Brands pay to advertise on the retailer's site, app or partner media, and the retailer uses its own shopper and sales data to target ads and report results.

Is Amazon a retail media network?

Yes. Amazon Ads sells on-site sponsored ads and off-site Amazon DSP, and it is the largest network for most online brands.

How is retail media different from regular digital advertising?

The retailer sees what shoppers buy, so results can be tied to actual purchases instead of only clicks. The trade-off is that the network reports on its own results, so it pays to ask how sales are attributed.

How much does it cost to advertise on a retail media network?

It depends on the network and format. On-site ads are usually bought per click with no setup fee on many networks, and off-site ads per thousand impressions. Check each network's current page for minimums.

Do small brands need retail media?

Often yes, in its simplest form. Sponsored ads on the marketplace where you already sell are the usual starting point. Off-site buying makes more sense once the basics are working.
