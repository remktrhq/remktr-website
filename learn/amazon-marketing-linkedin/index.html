Why the search results are confusing

Search "amazon marketing linkedin" and you get two things: a wall of job postings with titles like "Amazon PPC Strategist" and "Media Manager, Amazon Brand Marketing," and Amazon's own corporate LinkedIn page. Neither tells you what the job actually is. That's the real question buried in this search — what does "Amazon marketing" mean, and which version of it do you need?

The confusion is structural, not accidental. LinkedIn's search treats "Amazon" as a company name and "marketing" as a function, so it returns every posting containing both words — Amazon's internal sales staff, brand-side PPC hires, agency roles, and DSP media buyers, all mixed into one feed. They are not the same job.

The three jobs hiding under one search term

Pull apart the postings and three distinct roles emerge:

  • Amazon-side sales and account roles. Titles like "Sales Account Manager, Sponsored Ads" or "Account Manager, Telecom" are Amazon employees. Their job is to sell Amazon's ad inventory to brands and agencies — they work for Amazon, not for you, and their incentive is ad spend growth on Amazon's platform, not your ROAS.
  • Brand-side self-serve PPC roles. "Amazon PPC Strategist," "Amazon Advertising Specialist," "Amazon Ads Specialist" — hired by manufacturers and agencies to manage Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display bids inside Seller or Vendor Central. Day-to-day work is keyword harvesting, bid adjustment, and budget pacing.
  • DSP and media-buying roles. "Media Manager, Amazon Brand Marketing, XCM," "Paid Media Manager, Amazon Supply Chain Services," "Senior Paid Media Strategist (Amazon Ads)" — closer to programmatic display and video, on and off Amazon, bought through a DSP seat, and reconciled against sales data rather than click counts alone.

A single "Amazon Marketing Manager" req can legitimately mean any of the three, or all three stapled onto one salary.

A worked example: what 'good' actually looks like

Here's why the distinction matters. Sponsored Ads report on last-click: a shopper clicks, buys inside the attribution window, and the click gets full credit — even if that shopper was already headed to the product. DSP campaigns, measured correctly, get tested against a holdout group that saw no ads at all, so the lift is the gap between the two groups, not a click count.

We measure our own DSP book this way. Across 30 of our advertisers in July 2026, the portfolio delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole book, not cherry-picked from the best line item. That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. If you've seen a $0.41 CPC quoted for Amazon DSP elsewhere, that figure is online-video only — not the blended cost across formats, and quoting it as the whole book overstates what a typical campaign costs.

Blended cost per acquisition across that book was $5.49 on 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases came from shoppers new to the brand. That new-to-brand figure is the one most job postings — and most vendors — never mention, because last-click attribution can't produce it. It takes a Marketing Cloud table joining DSP exposure to purchase history.

The common mistake: hiring one title to do three jobs

The mistake we see most, and have made ourselves early on an account, is treating the PPC hire and the DSP hire as interchangeable. A Seller Central bid manager optimizes for search rank and immediate conversion; a DSP media buyer optimizes for reach against a defined audience and measures incrementality weeks later. Asking one person to do both, on top of AMC queries and holdout design, is asking for a skill set that doesn't really exist in one person.

The mirror version happens on the hiring side: a job description that lists Sponsored Products bid strategy and DSP audience buying as the same bullet point. The tools, the seat access, and the reporting cadence are different enough that people excellent at one are usually mediocre at the other.

If the hire — or the agency — isn't producing results

Before assuming the person or the platform is the problem, check three things:

  • Is DSP being measured against Sponsored Ads, or against nothing? If both channels claim last-click credit for the same sale, you're double-counting, and the reported ROAS on either one is inflated.
  • Does the seat actually have Amazon Marketing Cloud access? Without it there's no way to see overlap or new-to-brand rate — only the top-line numbers Amazon's dashboard hands you.
  • Is there a holdout or matched control anywhere in the plan? If every performance claim traces back to a click, the incrementality question was never answered — it was assumed.

Common questions

Is an 'Amazon Advertising Specialist' job the same as working for Amazon?

No — unless the listing is posted directly by Amazon, these are brand-side or agency roles managing Sponsored Ads bids inside Seller or Vendor Central. Amazon's own ad-sales staff carry titles like "Sales Account Manager, Sponsored Ads" and work for Amazon's ad business, selling inventory rather than managing your account.

What's the actual difference between Amazon Ads and Amazon DSP?

Amazon Ads (Sponsored Products, Brands, Display) is self-serve, keyword and placement bidding managed inside Seller/Vendor Central, mostly on-Amazon. Amazon DSP is seat-based programmatic buying, on and off Amazon, aimed at reach and remarketing, and it's the layer where incrementality can actually be measured against a holdout group rather than assumed from a click.

Can one person handle both Sponsored Ads and DSP?

On a small account, yes, out of necessity. Past a certain spend level the two need different daily attention — bid pacing versus audience and measurement design — and most people strong at one aren't strong at both. That's usually the point brands split the function or bring in a DSP-specific team.

How do I tell if an Amazon marketing hire or agency is actually working?

Ask for the new-to-brand rate and whether it's measured with a holdout or matched control, not just a reported ROAS. A number that only ever comes from last-click attribution can't tell you whether the ad caused the sale or just claimed credit for one that was coming anyway.

What is Amazon Marketing Cloud and why does it keep coming up?

AMC is the clean-room reporting layer where DSP and Sponsored Ads exposure can be joined to actual purchase events without one channel's last-click claim overwriting the other's. It's what makes a new-to-brand or incrementality number trustworthy instead of assumed.
