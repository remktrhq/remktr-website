What Are Amazon Marketing Emails — And How Do You Actually Stop Them?

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1501 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon marketing emails”
The short answer

Amazon marketing emails are promotional messages Amazon sends to shoppers — deals, recommendations, new releases — controlled through the Communications Preference Center, separate from order and subscription emails. Brands sometimes use the same phrase for on-Amazon tools like Manage Your Customer Engagement or DSP remarketing, which work differently and aren't inbox email at all.

Three different things people mean by "Amazon marketing email"

Most people typing this phrase into a search bar want one thing: to stop Amazon emailing them about deals. But the phrase actually covers three separate systems, and mixing them up is why the fix so often doesn't work.

  • Amazon's own promotional emails — deals, product recommendations, "you might also like" nudges. This is what the setting actually controls.
  • Transactional and subscription emails — order confirmations, shipping updates, Subscribe & Save reminders, Prime renewal notices. These live under a different toggle and won't stop no matter what you do to the marketing setting.
  • What brands mean by it — sellers and marketers sometimes use "Amazon marketing email" loosely to describe Manage Your Customer Engagement (MYCE), Amazon's tool for messaging your own followers, or the remarketing Amazon runs through its DSP. Neither one lands in an inbox the way a Klaviyo campaign does.

If you're a shopper, the next section is your answer. If you're a brand trying to figure out what Amazon offers instead of email marketing, skip to the section on what brands actually control.

How to turn off Amazon's marketing emails

Amazon's own help page gets the destination right but skips the part that trips people up: there isn't one switch, there are several, and they live in different places.

  • Open the Communications Preference Center from your account settings.
  • Under General Settings, expand Email and check "Do not send me any marketing email for now." Save.
  • If you also get catalogs or deal flyers by post, expand Mail in the same center and uncheck the marketing mail option separately — it's a different setting, not a side effect of the first one.
  • Anything tied to a specific program — Subscribe & Save reminders, Prime renewal notices, seller or Vine emails — is managed on that program's own page, not in the general preference center.

Amazon tells you it can take one to five business days to take effect. That's real, not a brush-off — the setting propagates through their send systems, it doesn't apply instantly.

When it doesn't work: the usual reasons

If marketing emails keep arriving after you've unsubscribed, it's almost always one of four things, in order of likelihood:

  • You're still inside the five-day window. Wait it out before assuming the setting failed.
  • You have more than one Amazon account or email address — a second household profile, an old account from a name change, a business account — and you only updated one of them.
  • What you're getting isn't actually marketing. Order updates, delivery notices, and return confirmations are transactional and will keep coming regardless — that's by design, not a bug.
  • You unsubscribed from a program email, not the general marketing setting. Clicking unsubscribe at the bottom of a Subscribe & Save reminder doesn't touch the general marketing checkbox, and vice versa.

There's no support ticket that fixes this faster than checking which of the four it actually is.

What brands actually control instead of "email marketing"

Amazon doesn't sell brands an email marketing platform. There's no Klaviyo-style tool where you build a list and send campaigns to Amazon customers directly. What exists instead is Manage Your Customer Engagement, which lets a brand message shoppers who've chosen to follow it — a grooming or cookware brand with a loyal repeat-purchase base might use it for launch announcements, but the list is small, opt-in, and Amazon-controlled, not owned the way an email list is.

The channel that actually reaches new and existing customers at scale on Amazon is DSP — display and video served against Amazon's own shopper data, on and off the platform. It's not email, but it's the closest thing Amazon has to owned-audience remarketing, and the numbers behind it are worth knowing if you're going to evaluate whether it's working. Across 30 advertisers in one DSP portfolio in July 2026, the book delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole set of accounts, not the best-performing line item pulled out to make a slide. That same book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. If you've seen a $0.41 CPC quoted for Amazon DSP somewhere, that figure is online-video only — it's not what the whole book pays, and quoting it as a blended number is a common way this category gets misrepresented. Blended cost per acquisition on that book was $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, and one in five of those purchases — 20.1% — came from a shopper new to the brand.

The mistake worth naming: judging this on last click

The most common error — and one we've made ourselves before building around it — is measuring display and remarketing the same way you'd measure a search ad: last click, one attribution window, done. Display rarely wins the last click. It sits earlier in the path, and last-click reporting will make it look like it did nothing even when it moved the sale.

The only honest way to check is to stop counting the same purchase twice across DSP and sponsored ads, and to compare against a holdout or matched control group rather than a click. That's a reconciliation problem, not a creative problem, and it's the reason so many brands conclude display "doesn't work" when what actually happened is they never separated it from search in the first place.

Common questions

Why am I still getting Amazon marketing emails after unsubscribing?

Give it the full one to five business days Amazon states before assuming it failed. If it's been longer, check whether you have a second account or email address on file, and confirm what you're actually receiving is marketing and not an order or shipping notice, which won't stop through this setting.

Does turning off marketing emails stop Amazon ads from following me around the web?

No. That's a separate system — Amazon's DSP serves display and video remarketing based on shopping behavior, and it isn't controlled by the email preference center. Ad personalization has its own opt-out, found under Your Ads Privacy Choices.

Is 'Amazon marketing email' the same thing as Amazon Marketing Cloud?

No, and the similar names cause real confusion. Amazon Marketing Cloud (AMC) is a clean-room analytics environment advertisers use to measure ad performance across DSP and sponsored ads without double-counting. It has nothing to do with the emails a shopper receives.

Can a brand send its own marketing emails through Amazon?

Only to its existing followers, through Manage Your Customer Engagement — there's no tool for building or emailing a broader list the way you would with a standalone email platform. For reaching new and existing customers at scale on Amazon, DSP is the channel brands actually use, even though it isn't email.

What counts as 'marketing' versus 'transactional' Amazon email?

Marketing email is anything promotional — deals, recommendations, new releases. Transactional email is anything tied to an order or account action you've already taken — confirmations, shipping updates, return receipts. Amazon keeps these on separate systems by design, so opting out of one never touches the other.
