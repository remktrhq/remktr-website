What Rigorous TV Attribution Looks Like When a CFO Has to Accept It

Updated 2026-11-01 · 1552 words · Written against what currently ranked for “what does rigorous tv attribution look like that a cfo would accept?”
The short answer

Rigorous TV attribution a CFO would accept is a controlled test, not a dashboard: a holdout or geo-lift read that reports incremental cost per acquisition, a stated confidence level, and what revenue would be lost if the spend stopped, with measured, modelled and attributed figures labelled separately.

What a CFO is actually testing for

A finance leader reading a TV report is not asking how the spot performed. They are asking one question: if we cut this line, does revenue fall, by how much, and how sure are you? Streaming TV is the hardest channel to answer it for, because the viewer rarely clicks. Most of its credit comes from view-through matching, which is exactly the part a sceptical reader will question first.

So rigorous does not mean a bigger dashboard. It means the report can survive that one question. Four tests of that:

  • Counterfactual: is there a group that did not see the ads to compare against?
  • Incremental, not attributed: is the headline cost per acquisition derived from that comparison, rather than from the platform crediting its own ads?
  • Stated confidence: does every number say whether it was measured, modelled or merely attributed?
  • Decision-ready: does the report say what to do with the budget?

Why the standard marketing report doesn't land with finance

ROAS, new-to-brand percentage and attribution model comparisons are the vocabulary marketing teams use with each other. They are mostly the wrong vocabulary for a finance conversation. A ROAS figure answers a narrow question, what got credited, and a sophisticated finance leader will ask the incrementality question straight back, because ROAS alone has never told them what they need for a budget decision.

For reference, across 30 advertisers we manage on Amazon DSP in July 2026, the portfolio delivered a 6.04x return on ad spend and a $5.49 blended cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases. Those are real figures, and they are attributed figures. They tell a CFO what the platform credited, not what would have happened without the spend, and a report that stops there invites the follow-up it cannot answer.

The three numbers worth leading with

First, incremental cost per acquisition. This is not the attributed CPA. It is the number derived from an actual holdout or geo-lift test, because it reflects what a genuinely new customer costs rather than what the platform's own reporting claims.

Second, a stated confidence level attached to that number. A properly powered test earns high confidence. A directional signal such as new-to-brand rate earns medium. An unvalidated attributed figure is low confidence, and worth saying so plainly. As a reference point for the directional tier, 20.1% of the attributed purchases in the same 30-advertiser July 2026 book came from shoppers new to the brand: useful as a signal, not a measured outcome.

Third, the answer to the cut-test question directly: based on the evidence available, what would happen to revenue if this specific spend stopped, framed as a range rather than a false-precision point estimate.

A worked translation, from marketing numbers to finance numbers

You can run this calculation on your own TV line with four inputs from your own data: monthly spend, attributed orders, the incremental lift from a test, and the margin per order. The illustration below uses placeholders, not results.

  • Step 1, find the incremental share. A geo-lift or holdout test gives you a lift: the share of attributed sales that would not have happened without the ads.
  • Step 2, restate the orders. Incremental orders = attributed orders x incremental share.
  • Step 3, restate the cost. Incremental CPA = monthly spend / incremental orders.
  • Step 4, compare. Set incremental CPA beside the acquisition cost of the channels finance already tracks, and beside the margin per order.

The effect is always in the same direction: because the incremental share is below 100%, incremental CPA is higher than attributed CPA. That is not bad news to hide. It is the number a CFO can put next to every other channel on the same footing, and it is the number that makes the rest of your report believable.

What to admit is uncertain, and why that builds trust

A CFO who has sat through enough marketing reviews has learned to distrust reports with no visible uncertainty, because real measurement always has some. State plainly which numbers are directly measured (a completed incrementality test), which are modelled or estimated (a marketing mix model coefficient, a projected lift from a partial-duration test), and which are simply attributed and unvalidated.

For TV specifically, also state the test design: how the exposed and unexposed groups were chosen, how long the test ran, and who computed the result. Where the party buying the media also designs and computes the test, say so. That is a fair question for any vendor to answer, including us, and writing the test design into the statement of work before launch removes it from the argument later.

The common mistake, including ours

The mistake is presenting a blended, attributed portfolio ROAS as the headline number in a finance-facing report, because it is the biggest, most impressive-sounding figure available. It is the number that invites the sharpest question a finance leader can ask, would this revenue exist without the spend, and it answers that question badly.

We are not exempt. Attributed ROAS is the easy number to lead with, and a report built around it depends on nobody asking the obvious follow-up. A report built around incremental CPA and a confidence level answers that exact question up front, which means it survives the most sceptical reader in the room.

Formatting the report itself

Lead with the incremental CPA and confidence level in the first line, not after a page of channel-by-channel breakdown. A CFO reading quickly should get the answer to the cut-test question before anything else. Follow with the supporting evidence: what was tested, when, and at what scale, so the headline number is traceable rather than asserted. Save marketing-native metrics such as ROAS, new-to-brand and CTR for an appendix, available for anyone who wants the detail but not required reading for the decision.

A one-page checklist before you send it:

  • Is the headline number incremental, and is it labelled as such?
  • Does every figure carry measured, modelled or attributed next to it?
  • Is the test window, design and scale stated?
  • Is there a plain sentence on what happens to revenue if the spend stops?
  • Is there a recommendation: reallocate, retest at a different scale, or hold for a longer read?

When the honest answer is bad news for finance

If a test shows that a channel finance has been funding on the strength of a strong attributed ROAS produces little incremental lift, say so plainly and immediately, with the incremental CPA attached, rather than softening the finding or waiting for a more favourable period. A CFO who learns that a channel's real incrementality was known and unreported for months loses more trust than one who hears a disappointing number promptly, with the confidence level and a clear recommendation for what to do next.

Which one you should actually pick

Any marketing team can build this translation layer itself: it is a reporting-format decision, not a technical capability. reMKTR, which runs Amazon DSP as a service, builds client reports around incremental CPA and stated confidence levels, drawing on the discipline of Full Circle (100+ brands managed, $500M+ in Amazon revenue managed).

What to do with this

Whoever runs your DSP, a partner or your own team, ask them two things: how much of DSP's attributed sales also had a sponsored ads click, and how they would hold out part of the audience in month one. Back whoever answers with a method rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What does rigorous TV attribution look like to a CFO?

A controlled test with a comparison group that did not see the ads, a headline incremental cost per acquisition derived from it, a stated confidence level on every figure, and a plain answer to what happens to revenue if the spend stops.

What's the single most important number to lead with in a CFO report?

Incremental cost per acquisition, derived from an actual test rather than platform attribution. It translates most directly into a budget decision a finance leader can act on.

Should I ever show attributed ROAS to a CFO?

It is fine as supporting context, but it should not be the headline figure, and it should be labelled as attributed rather than incremental so the distinction isn't lost.

How do I explain uncertainty without looking unprepared?

Attach an explicit confidence level to every figure: measured, modelled or attributed-only. A report that names its own uncertainty consistently reads as more credible, not less.

What if I don't have a completed incrementality test yet for a given channel?

Say so directly, present the best available directional signal, such as new-to-brand rate, labelled at its actual confidence level, and note when a proper test is scheduled.
