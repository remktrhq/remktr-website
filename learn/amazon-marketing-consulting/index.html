What Amazon marketing consulting actually covers

Amazon marketing consulting is an umbrella term for paid help improving how a brand performs on Amazon. It splits into three jobs that get bundled together more often than they should: listing and creative work (titles, images, A+ content, storefronts), advertising management (Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display, and DSP), and account operations (inventory, compliance, account health). A consultant might do one of these or all three under a single contract.

The engagement itself ranges from a single paid audit, a report and a list of fixes with no ongoing work, to a full managed service where the consultant runs your account month to month. Fee structures follow the same range: a flat project fee for an audit, a monthly retainer for ongoing listing or account work, and a percentage of ad spend, sometimes with a base fee layered on top, for advertising management. None of these structures is inherently better than another. The mismatch that causes problems is paying a flat retainer for work that scales with your spend, or paying a percentage for work that does not.

Most pages selling this service describe the menu (audits, PPC, listing optimization, storefronts) without saying how those pieces get measured against each other. That is the part worth spending time on, because it is where the money actually moves.

Amazon brand consulting vs Amazon marketing consulting

People search for both phrases and often mean the same thing, but the emphasis differs. Marketing consulting usually centres on traffic and conversion: advertising, listings and creative. Brand consulting usually centres on the longer game: how the brand is presented across the storefront and A+ content, which products to lead with, how Brand Registry protections are used, and how paid media builds awareness among shoppers who have not bought yet.

The practical test is the same for both. Ask what the consultant will be accountable for, in a number, and how it will be measured. A brand project with no measurable outcome is a workshop. A marketing project with no brand view can win this month's sales and quietly weaken next quarter's.

How an engagement runs, and what you should receive at each stage

Every legitimate engagement, regardless of vendor, moves through the same four stages. Use them to check any proposal against what it should contain at each step, not just what it promises at the end.

  • Audit and discovery: the consultant pulls your catalog, ad account and account health data and gives you a written list of specific problems ranked by revenue impact, not a generic checklist.
  • Strategy: fixes are mapped to your goals and budget, with named metrics and an agreed baseline.
  • Execution: listings are rewritten, campaigns restructured and operational issues fixed, with a change log so cause and effect can be traced.
  • Measurement and reporting: performance is tracked against the baseline, covering the whole account rather than the best line item.

The stage most consultants skip is the baseline. If nobody wrote down what your cost per acquisition, ACoS or organic rank were before work started, you have no way to know whether next quarter's numbers are progress or noise. Ask for the baseline in writing before you sign anything.

How Amazon consulting fees work, and how to test them

Fees come in four common shapes: a flat fee for an audit, a monthly retainer, a percentage of ad spend (often with a base fee), or a custom quote. Each fits a different kind of work, so the useful question is not which is cheapest but which one moves with the work being done.

  • Flat project fee suits a defined deliverable such as an audit or a listing rewrite.
  • Monthly retainer suits steady, predictable work such as content and account operations.
  • Percentage of ad spend suits advertising management, where the workload grows with the budget. It is a normal, widely used model, and reMKTR charges this way too.
  • Custom quote is common for larger scopes. Many providers quote on a call, so compare scope line by line rather than headline to headline.

To test any percentage, do the multiplication yourself. At a 10% fee, $10,000 of monthly ad spend is $1,000 a month in fees. Then ask whether the rate changes as spend grows, whether there is a base fee on top, and whether you get written notice of any fee change. For reference, Dr. PPC, our autonomous ad management software, publishes its price: $300 a month plus 2% of ad spend, with the first 30 days for $1, Orbit included and no contract. A worked example: reading a consultant's numbers correctly

Definitions matter here. Say a consultant hands you a portfolio result. For reference, reMKTR's own Amazon DSP book across 30 advertisers in July 2026 delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, and a $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, with 20.1% of those purchases coming from shoppers new to the brand. That is a whole-book number: every account, every channel, averaged together, including the accounts that did not perform as well as the best one. It is not a promise about what your account will do.

Now compare that to a common habit: quoting the best line item instead of the book. A $0.41 CPC sounds better than $1.42, and it is not technically a lie. It is the online-video placement only, not the whole book. Both numbers can be true at once. The question to ask any consultant showing you a CPC, CPA or ROAS is simple: is this blended across everything you are paying for, or is this the best channel picked out of the mix? If they cannot answer immediately, the number is decoration.

The same discipline applies to attribution. Last-click reporting tells you a sale happened after an ad was clicked. It cannot tell you whether that sale would have happened anyway. Proving incrementality needs a holdout or a matched control, not a bigger attribution window, and it is worth asking who designs and computes the test and whether it is written into the statement of work.

Questions to ask before you hire, and who each option suits

Use this checklist with any consultant or agency, including us:

  • What single number are you accountable for, and how is it measured?
  • Will you record a baseline in writing before work starts?
  • Are reported results blended across the whole account or selected from the best channel?
  • Who does the work, and who checks it against the other channels?
  • What happens to fees as spend grows, and will I get written notice of any change?
  • What is the term, the notice period, and who owns the account data if we part ways?

The mistakes that actually cost money, including ours

  • Grading on ACOS alone. ACOS ignores organic sales lift and new-to-brand contribution. A campaign with a bad-looking ACOS can still be the reason a listing's organic rank improved that month.
  • Trusting last-click for incrementality. We have made this mistake ourselves. Last-click attribution was never built to prove whether spend added anything; it just assigns credit to whichever ad ran last. It took reconciling DSP and sponsored ads in one place, so a sale is not counted twice, to answer the question honestly.
  • Paying for everything under one roof without asking who touches what. A bundled retainer is only efficient if the person managing DSP campaigns checks results against the person managing Sponsored Products. Otherwise two teams can claim credit for the same sale.
  • Hiring for a title, not a metric. Consultant, agency and specialist are titles. Ask what number someone is accountable for before asking what they call themselves.

When the numbers turn out to be bad news, isolate the variable before changing consultants or budget. Check that a baseline existed and was measured the same way both times, that reporting is blended rather than selected, and whether anything on Amazon's side, such as a category-wide CPC rise or a policy change, shifted independently of the work. A consultant worth keeping will tell you a tactic failed rather than reframe the metric that measures it.

Common questions

What is Amazon marketing consulting?

It is paid expert help improving how a brand performs on Amazon, covering listings and creative, advertising, and account operations. It can be a one-off audit, a monthly retainer, or a percentage of ad spend, and many providers combine all three.

What does an Amazon marketing consultant actually do day to day?

It depends which piece they are hired for. A listing consultant rewrites titles, bullets and A+ content and does keyword research. An advertising consultant builds and adjusts Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display or DSP campaigns. An operations consultant handles inventory, compliance paperwork and account health. Most agencies sell some combination of the three under one contract.

How much does Amazon marketing consulting cost?

Pricing follows one of four structures: a flat fee for a one-time audit, a monthly retainer for ongoing listing or account work, a percentage of ad spend for advertising management (sometimes with a base fee), or a custom quote. Rates vary by scope and vendor, so check current pricing directly rather than trusting a number from a review site, and always ask whether the percentage is disclosed and whether it changes as spend grows.

Is an Amazon marketing consultant different from an Amazon agency?

Not consistently. Consultant sometimes implies advice without execution, like an audit or strategy document, while agency implies ongoing hands-on management. In practice many companies selling Amazon marketing consulting do both under one contract, so ask what is included rather than relying on the label.

Do I need a consultant if I already manage PPC in-house?

Not necessarily. If your in-house team has time to track blended CPA and ACOS across the whole account, test creative and keep up with ad format changes, consulting adds the most value where you are missing bandwidth or a specific skill, such as DSP setup, incrementality measurement or a category-specific launch.
