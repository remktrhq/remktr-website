What Amazon marketing consulting actually covers

Amazon marketing consulting is an umbrella term for paid help improving how a brand performs on Amazon. It splits into three jobs that get bundled together more often than they should: listing and creative work (titles, images, A+ content, storefronts), advertising management (Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display, and DSP), and account operations (inventory, compliance, account health). A consultant might do one of these or all three under a single contract.

The engagement itself ranges from a single paid audit — a report and a list of fixes, no ongoing work — to a full managed service where the consultant runs your account month to month. Fee structures follow the same range: a flat project fee for an audit, a monthly retainer for ongoing listing or account work, and a percentage of ad spend, sometimes with a base fee layered on top, for advertising management. None of these structures is inherently better than another. The mismatch that causes problems is paying a flat retainer for work that scales with your spend, or paying a percentage for work that doesn't.

Most pages selling this service describe the menu — audits, PPC, listing optimization, storefronts — without saying how those pieces get measured against each other. That's the part worth spending time on, because it's where the money actually moves.

How an engagement actually runs

Every legitimate engagement, regardless of vendor, moves through the same four stages. Use the stages below to check any proposal against what it should contain at each step, not just what it promises at the end.

The stage most consultants skip is the baseline. If nobody wrote down what your CPA, ACOS, or organic rank were before work started, you have no way to know whether next quarter's numbers are progress or noise. Ask for the baseline in writing before you sign anything.

A worked example — reading a consultant's numbers correctly

Here's where the definitions matter. Say a consultant hands you a portfolio result: 6.04x return on ad spend across 30 advertiser accounts, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, and a $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 purchases, with 20.1% of those purchases coming from shoppers new to the brand. That's a whole-book number — every account, every channel, averaged together, including the accounts that didn't perform as well as the best one.

Now compare that to a common trick: quoting the best line item instead of the book. A $0.41 CPC sounds better than $1.42, and it isn't technically a lie — it's just the online-video placement only, not the whole account. Both numbers can be true at once. The question to ask any consultant showing you a CPC, CPA, or ROAS is simple: is this blended across everything you're paying for, or is this the best channel cherry-picked out of the mix? If they can't answer immediately, the number is decoration.

The same discipline applies to attribution. Last-click reporting will tell you a sale happened after an ad was clicked. It cannot tell you whether that sale would have happened anyway. Proving incrementality — whether the spend actually added something — needs a holdout or a matched control, not a bigger attribution window.

What to do when the numbers are bad news

Sometimes the audit finds the CPA is rising, the ACOS didn't move, or the growth plan produced nothing but bigger invoices. That's not a reason to fire everyone in a panic — it's a reason to isolate the variable. Before changing consultants, changing budget, or scrapping the whole strategy, check three things in order: did a baseline exist and was it measured the same way both times; is the reporting blended or cherry-picked; and has anything on Amazon's side — a category-wide CPC increase, a policy change, a competitor's price move — shifted independently of the work being done.

If the fix genuinely didn't work, say so and ask why before scaling budget behind it anyway. A consultant worth keeping will tell you a tactic failed rather than reframe the metric that measures it. That's rarer than it should be.

The mistakes that actually cost money — including ours

  • Grading on ACOS alone. ACOS ignores organic sales lift and new-to-brand contribution. A campaign with a "bad" ACOS can still be the reason a listing's organic rank improved that month.
  • Trusting last-click for incrementality. We've made this mistake ourselves. Last-click attribution was never built to prove whether spend added anything — it just assigns credit to whichever ad ran last. It took reconciling display and sponsored ads in the same place, so one sale doesn't get counted twice, to actually answer the question honestly.
  • Paying for everything under one roof without asking who touches what. A bundled retainer sounds efficient. It's only efficient if the person managing DSP campaigns checks their results against the person managing Sponsored Products — otherwise two teams can end up claiming credit for the same sale.
  • Hiring for a title, not a metric. Consultant, agency, specialist — these are titles. Ask what number someone is accountable for, and how it's measured, before asking what they call themselves.

Common questions

What does an Amazon marketing consultant actually do day to day?

Depends which piece they're hired for. A listing consultant rewrites titles, bullets, and A+ content and does keyword research. An advertising consultant builds and adjusts Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display, or DSP campaigns. An operations consultant handles inventory, compliance paperwork, and account health. Most agencies sell some combination of the three under one contract.

How much does Amazon marketing consulting cost?

Pricing follows one of four structures: a flat fee for a one-time audit, a monthly retainer for ongoing listing or account work, a percentage of ad spend for advertising management, sometimes with a base fee layered on top, or a custom quote. Rates vary by scope and vendor, so check current pricing directly rather than trusting a number from a review site — and always ask whether the percentage is disclosed, since a hidden point on a large budget adds up fast.

Is an Amazon marketing consultant different from an Amazon agency?

Not consistently — the terms overlap in how they're used. "Consultant" sometimes implies advice without execution, like an audit or a strategy document, while "agency" implies ongoing hands-on management. In practice most companies selling Amazon marketing consulting today do both under the same contract, so ask what's included rather than relying on the label.

How long before I see results?

Listing and content changes can show movement in organic rank within a few weeks. Advertising changes usually need four to eight weeks of stable spend to read cleanly, because the platform's own optimization needs volume to work against. Anything claiming dramatic results inside the first month is either measuring against a very low baseline or measuring the wrong thing.

Do I need a consultant if I already manage PPC in-house?

Not necessarily. If your in-house team has time to track blended CPA and ACOS across the whole account, test creative, and keep up with ad format changes, consulting adds the most value where you're missing bandwidth or a specific skill — DSP setup, incrementality measurement, or a category-specific launch — not where you already have both covered.
