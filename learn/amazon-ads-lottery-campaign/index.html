Why Your Amazon Ads Campaign Feels Like a Lottery

The short answer

What's actually happening when a campaign looks random

Amazon's ad auctions — Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and DSP — are real-time and bid-based. Winning an individual impression is never guaranteed, even at the top bid; you're competing against other advertisers' bids on that exact impression, in that exact moment, and losing some of those auctions is normal, not a bug.

The bigger driver of the "lottery" feeling is what happens to brand-new keywords, ASINs, or campaigns. Amazon's system doesn't know yet which placements convert for you, so early on it spreads spend across a wider set of keywords and placements to collect data — an explore phase, before it exploits what's working. Two campaigns launched the same day, with the same budget and near-identical products, can get very different early results purely because the algorithm chose to test different things on each one.

That's not manipulation and it's not random in the sense of a coin flip with no logic behind it. It's a system gathering signal before it optimizes. The mistake is reading that gathering phase as a verdict on the campaign.

A worked example: why the numbers swing wildly in week one

Say a new Sponsored Products campaign gets 40 clicks in its first three days and produces 1 sale. That's a 2.5% conversion rate — it looks broken. Run the same campaign another four days, get 40 more clicks, and land 3 sales — 7.5% conversion rate, suddenly it looks great. Nothing about the product, the bid, or the listing changed. The sample size just moved from useless to slightly-less-useless. At 40 clicks, the margin of error on a conversion rate estimate is enormous; one extra sale swings the headline number by five points.

This is exactly why per-line-item, day-one reads are close to worthless, and why the numbers only become trustworthy at real volume. Across 30 of our own advertisers in July 2026, the book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 CPC, producing 57,137 attributed purchases at a $5.49 blended CPA and a 6.04x return on ad spend measured across the whole book — not the best line item in it. That scale is what turns a number into a fact instead of a guess. A campaign with 40 clicks hasn't earned the right to a verdict yet.

The practical rule: don't act on conversion rate or ACOS until you have at least a few dozen conversions in the bucket you're judging, not a few dozen clicks. Clicks without conversions tell you almost nothing about profitability.

When the number really is bad news

Sometimes the campaign isn't noisy, it's actually broken, and patience just wastes budget. Here's how to tell the difference before you touch anything:

  • Check the sample first. Fewer than roughly 100 clicks on the segment you're judging — a keyword, an ASIN, a placement — means you don't have a number yet, you have a rumor.
  • Check delivery, not just performance. A campaign spending a fraction of its daily budget every day is capped by bid or targeting, not by demand — that's a delivery problem, not a conversion problem.
  • Check the listing before the bid. If clicks are healthy but conversion is flat at real volume, the ad isn't the problem — price, images, reviews, or stock status usually is.
  • Check eligibility. Suppressed listings, out-of-stock ASINs, and buy-box loss will quietly kill a campaign's numbers while looking, from the dashboard, exactly like a bad bid.

If you've ruled those out and the campaign has real volume — hundreds of clicks, real spend — and it's still unprofitable, that's a genuine result. Don't keep feeding it in the hope it was noise. At that point the fix is a bid, budget, or targeting change, not more patience.

The mistake almost everyone makes — including us

The most common error is pausing a new campaign after two or three days of bad-looking data, relaunching it fresh, and repeating the cycle every week. Each relaunch resets the explore phase, so the campaign never accumulates enough volume to give you a real number. You end up permanently stuck in the noisiest part of the process, judging a system that hasn't finished gathering data yet.

We've made this mistake ourselves, early in a new ASIN launch — pulling a campaign on day three because ACOS looked ugly, when the underlying sample was a dozen clicks. The fix wasn't a smarter bid, it was a minimum look-back window before anyone is allowed to touch a new campaign's settings.

The opposite mistake is just as common: a keyword spikes early purely on noise, someone dumps budget behind it because it looks like a winner, and performance quietly reverts to the mean two weeks later. A lucky early result is still noise. It just looks like a good kind of noise.

The same noise shows up in DSP — and why last-click can't settle it

Programmatic display and video auctions on Amazon DSP work the same way underneath: every impression is a separate real-time auction, and reach and frequency across a campaign will vary week to week even with a stable budget and audience. That variance is normal, and it compounds with the attribution problem — last-click reporting will credit a display impression for a sale the shopper was already going to make, and it can't tell you which impressions actually changed a shopper's mind.

The way to answer that isn't a bigger dashboard, it's a holdout: run the campaign against a matched group that didn't see it, or reconcile DSP and sponsored ads together in Amazon Marketing Cloud so the same purchase isn't credited twice. That's the only way to know whether display added incremental sales or just repeated a sponsored ad's job at a higher CPM.

That doesn't change whether DSP is right for you; it just means don't judge it on day three either.

Which one you should actually pick

If you're running your own Sponsored Products or DSP campaigns, the fix here is patience and a minimum look-back window before you touch anything new — that costs nothing and suits most self-managed advertisers fine. If you need to know whether display spend is actually adding sales rather than restating them, that requires holdouts or AMC reconciliation, which is where a managed DSP practice like reMKTR's is built to help.

Common questions

Is Amazon Ads actually random, or does something control it?

It's not random in the arbitrary sense. Every impression is a discrete, bid-based auction, and new keywords or ASINs go through an explore phase where the system spreads spend to gather data before favoring what performs. It looks random because of thin sample sizes, not because there's no logic behind it.

How many clicks do I need before I can trust my ACOS or conversion rate?

There's no universal number, but treat anything under roughly 100 clicks on the specific keyword, ASIN, or placement you're judging as provisional. Judge on conversions, not clicks — a handful of conversions still carries a wide margin of error.

Should I pause a new campaign if it looks bad after three days?

Usually not, if the sample is thin. Pausing and relaunching resets the explore phase and guarantees you'll never get a stable read. If delivery is healthy and the loss is real at real volume, that's different — that's a signal worth acting on.

Does this 'lottery' feeling apply to Amazon DSP too?

Yes. DSP runs on real-time bidding across an ad exchange, so reach and frequency vary campaign to campaign even with identical budgets and audiences. It compounds with attribution: last-click can't tell you whether a display impression changed a shopper's mind or just repeated a sale that was already coming. Holdouts and AMC reconciliation answer that; last-click attribution doesn't.

What's the single biggest mistake advertisers make with new campaigns?

Acting on the first few days of data — either killing a campaign that looked bad on a dozen clicks, or dumping budget into one that spiked on noise. Both waste money on a number that hadn't finished forming yet.
