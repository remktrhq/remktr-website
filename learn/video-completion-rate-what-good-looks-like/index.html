Video Completion Rate: What Good Looks Like

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1300 words · Written against what currently ranked for “Video completion rate: what good looks like”
The short answer

Premium streaming and connected-TV completion rates generally run 90% or higher, with some ad-supported CTV inventory reported as high as 97.2% and non-skippable pre-roll up to 98.6%. Combined PC and mobile video completion averages closer to 62% — not because that creative is worse, but because skippable, feed-based formats give viewers an easy way out that non-skippable CTV simply doesn't offer.

The benchmarks, by environment and length

Industry-reported data puts premium streaming and CTV completion at 90%+ generally, with some ad-supported CTV platforms reported as high as 97.2% and non-skippable CTV pre-roll specifically at 98.6%. Length matters too: 30-second CTV ads reportedly complete at around 95.9%, 15-second ads at 93.9%, and ads of 10 seconds or under at 90.4% — the counterintuitive detail here is that on non-skippable CTV, longer creative can complete about as well as shorter creative, because the viewer was never going to have the option to skip either way. That's a real departure from the "shorter always completes better" assumption that holds on skippable formats.

It's worth naming what these benchmarks are and aren't. They're third-party industry figures, not numbers Amazon itself publishes as a guaranteed standard — useful as a planning reference, not as a contractual target. A campaign landing a few points below a reported benchmark isn't automatically underperforming; it's worth reading against the account's own trend over time as much as against an external number that describes the category broadly rather than any specific advertiser's audience.

Why CTV and mobile aren't comparable numbers

Combined PC and mobile video completion averages around 62% — a very different number from CTV's 90%+, and not because CTV creative is inherently more compelling. The mechanism is structural: CTV is overwhelmingly non-skippable, watched in a lean-back living-room context; mobile and PC video is frequently skippable, watched during active browsing with constant competing demands on attention. Comparing a 62% mobile VCR against a 95% CTV VCR as if one campaign is simply outperforming the other misreads what's actually being measured.

Screen size and device type correlate with these numbers without fully explaining them — a tablet playing a skippable video ad can still see completion behavior closer to mobile's than to CTV's, because what actually governs the number is whether the viewer has an easy exit, not the size of the screen they're watching on.

A worked example

A Prime Video campaign running 30-second creative at a 92% completion rate is performing in line with, or slightly below, the reported benchmark — a reasonable result, not a standout one. If the same brand's mobile OLV creative completes at 58%, that's close to the reported 62% average for that format, not evidence the OLV creative is failing relative to the CTV one. Reading both against their own format's benchmark, rather than against each other, is the only way to tell whether either is actually underperforming.

Completion rate is also the fastest available signal on a brand-new streaming line item — it's readable within days, well before enough time has passed for branded search or sales data to be trustworthy. Checking completion rate in week one, against the specific format's benchmark, is a standard first checkpoint before anything else on a new streaming flight, because it's the earliest point at which a genuine creative problem becomes visible.

When a low completion rate is a real signal

If CTV completion sits meaningfully below 85%, that's worth investigating — check length against the platform's accepted durations first, since a length outside what the placement is optimized for can hurt delivery and completion together, then check the opening seconds of the creative itself. If OLV completion sits well below the reported 62% average, check whether the creative front-loads its message assuming a skip option won't be used — OLV viewers who can skip generally do, within the first few seconds, if the hook doesn't hold.

Frequency is worth checking too, before touching the creative. A completion rate that starts strong and declines over the course of a flight, with no creative change, often points to over-frequency rather than a bad asset — the same household seeing the same ad for the fifth time in a week is simply less likely to watch it through, and the fix is a tighter frequency cap, not a new cut of the video.

Creative fatigue is a related, slower-moving cause worth checking separately from frequency: even at a reasonable frequency cap, a single creative run unchanged for months can see completion rate drift downward as an audience becomes generally familiar with it. Refreshing the creative concept periodically, not just the media plan around it, is part of maintaining a healthy completion rate over a long-running campaign.

The common mistake

The mistake is treating one universal VCR target across every format and length in a media plan, then judging every line item against it regardless of context. A 92% CTV completion rate and a 60% OLV completion rate can both be genuinely good results on their own terms — set the benchmark per format and length, not one number for the whole plan. reMKTR reports completion rate against the specific platform and duration benchmark for every line item, rather than a single blended completion target across a mixed-format campaign.

A second version of the mistake is chasing completion rate as the only metric that matters. A creative that completes at 96% but produces no branded search lift and no sales movement has held attention without doing anything with it — completion rate proves the ad was watched, not that it worked. Read it as a necessary early checkpoint, not the final scoreboard.

Which one you should actually pick

These benchmarks suit anyone setting or reading a completion-rate target and wanting to know whether a specific number is actually good or just looks good next to the wrong comparison. A brand running only one format doesn't need the cross-format nuance here — it only matters once a plan spans both CTV and OLV and someone starts comparing the two numbers directly.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 48.5% across the 47 brands above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is a 60% completion rate bad?

Not necessarily — it depends entirely on the format. For combined PC and mobile video, ~62% is close to the industry average, so 60% is roughly in line with expectations. For CTV, where 90%+ is typical, 60% would be a real signal worth investigating.

Does a longer video always complete worse?

On skippable formats, generally yes. On non-skippable CTV specifically, reported data shows 30-second ads completing about as well as 15-second ads, since the viewer never had a skip option to exercise either way.

Why does CTV complete so much better than mobile video?

Mainly format mechanics — CTV is overwhelmingly non-skippable and watched in a more attentive, lean-back context, while mobile and PC video is frequently skippable and watched during active, distracted browsing.

Should I use completion rate to compare a CTV campaign against an OLV campaign?

Not directly — compare each against its own format's benchmark instead. A direct comparison between the two numbers will make OLV look artificially weak, since the formats aren't measuring comparable viewer behavior.

How quickly can I read completion rate on a new campaign?

Within days, generally — it's one of the fastest available signals, readable well before there's enough time for branded search or sales data to be trustworthy, which is why it's worth checking first on any new streaming or video flight.
