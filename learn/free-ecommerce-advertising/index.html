What 'free ecommerce advertising' actually is

Free ecommerce advertising covers two different things people mean by this phrase, and mixing them up wastes time: organic promotion (posting, writing, emailing) and free infrastructure (store builders like Square's free plan, which charges a per-sale processing fee instead of a monthly one). Neither buys attention. Both require someone's time to run.

The organic list is genuinely useful and doesn't change much year to year: post on whichever platform your buyers already use, write content that answers real search questions, build an email list and mail it regularly, get listed in relevant directories, and use discount codes to convert people already looking. None of this is wrong. It's also not really 'advertising' in the sense of buying reach — it's distribution work you do yourself.

A free store builder is a different thing entirely: it's the shelf, not the traffic to the shelf. Square's free plan is a legitimate way to get a storefront live with no monthly fee, but it doesn't put a single new visitor in front of your products. You still need one of the organic tactics above, or a paid one, to get anyone there.

What 'free' actually costs — a worked example

Say a two-person team commits three hours a week to organic promotion: one blog post, five social updates, one email send. Over a quarter that's roughly 39 hours — not huge, but 39 hours not spent on product, ops, or customer service, and it usually takes several months before search rankings or list size move enough to count as a channel rather than a hobby. That's the real price of 'free': it's paid in time and in delayed payback, not in cash.

Compare that to what a tuned paid media book actually returns. Across 30 of our Amazon DSP advertisers in July 2026, the portfolio delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, running 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click — worth flagging because the $0.41 CPC figure that circulates in this category is online-video only, not the whole book. Blended cost per acquisition was $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand.

That's not 'paid beats free.' They solve different problems at different stages. Organic and free-tool tactics are the right first move when you have time and no proven margin to spend against yet. Paid media buys reach at a scale organic can't hit in the same window, and it can be measured for whether it actually added new customers — something free tactics are rarely measured for at all.

Where free tactics plateau, and the mistake almost everyone makes

Free channels stall for a predictable reason: you eventually reach everyone in your existing network, your existing list, and your existing search intent. Growth past that point needs new eyes — people who've never heard of the brand — and organic tactics are structurally weak at reaching people who aren't already looking for you or following someone who is.

The common mistake is treating a sales bump during a promotional push as proof the push worked, when some of those sales would have happened anyway. Last-click attribution credits whichever channel touched the order last, whether or not that channel caused it. We've made this exact mistake ourselves on the paid side — crediting display impressions for purchases that would have converted regardless — until we started measuring with holdouts and matched controls instead of trusting the click. The fix isn't more posting. It's a proper test: hold a segment out, compare it to a matched group that saw the promotion, and look at the gap.

If you've run the free playbook for a few months and sales have flattened, that's usually not evidence you're doing it wrong. It's evidence you've saturated the audience you can reach for free.

When to add paid media, and what to check first

Add paid spend when three things are true: the product has margin to support a customer acquisition cost, the free channels have genuinely plateaued rather than just started, and you have a way to measure incrementality rather than just watching sales during the campaign. Skip the third check and you'll overspend on a channel that's mostly getting credit for demand that already existed.

For Amazon sellers specifically, this usually means sponsored ads first — cost-efficient, intent-driven, easy to measure — with display or video (Amazon DSP) added later, once sponsored ads alone can't reach enough new shoppers. Display is genuinely hard to evaluate honestly because it sits on top of sponsored ads in the same funnel; without reconciling the two in one place, you'll double-count a sale both channels touched.

Which one you should actually pick

Free channels suit anyone pre-scale — no margin risk, real distribution skills built along the way. Square's free plan suits sellers who need a working storefront fast with no upfront cost. Paid media, including Amazon DSP, suits brands that have already outgrown organic reach and have margin to test — and only pays off once you can measure whether it's adding new customers or just taking credit for ones you'd have gotten anyway.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is free ecommerce advertising really free?

Not in labor. Every tactic on the free list — social posting, SEO content, email, forums — costs time to do well and usually takes months to become a reliable channel rather than an occasional bump. The cash cost is zero; the opportunity cost isn't.

What's the fastest free channel for a brand-new store?

There isn't a fast one, honestly. Email to a small list can convert quickly once people are on it, but building that list takes time regardless of channel. Anyone promising a fast free channel is usually describing a paid tactic with a free trial.

Should I use Square's free plan or build my own site?

Square's free plan is a reasonable choice if you want a storefront live with no upfront cost and don't mind a per-sale processing fee instead of a monthly one. It's infrastructure, not advertising — you'll still need one of the free or paid promotion tactics to send it traffic.

How do I know if my free promotion is actually working?

Watch for a trend across weeks, not a spike after one post. Sales during a push aren't proof the push caused them — some of those buyers would have converted anyway. A holdout comparison (some audience sees the promotion, a matched group doesn't) is the only honest way to know.

When does it make sense to pay for ecommerce advertising?

When your product has margin to support an acquisition cost, your free channels have genuinely plateaued rather than just started, and you have a way to measure whether the paid channel adds new customers instead of just taking credit for ones you'd have gotten anyway.
