The Formula Itself

Amazon conversion rate = (units ordered ÷ sessions) × 100. Divide the number of units a listing sold by the number of sessions it received, then multiply by 100 to get a percentage. That's the whole formula — the complexity is entirely in getting the two input numbers right.

Amazon doesn't actually call this "conversion rate" inside Seller Central. It's labeled Unit Session Percentage, and it sits next to a second, easily confused metric called Order Session Percentage. The two diverge whenever a customer buys more than one unit in a single order — six batteries in one order counts as six units against one order. Most sellers only ever look at Unit Session Percentage and call it their conversion rate, which is fine as long as you're consistent about which one you're reading and comparing.

Where to Find Sessions and Orders in Seller Central

Both inputs live in one report: Business Reports → Detail Page Sales and Traffic, pulled either by ASIN or by Parent Item if you manage variations. The columns you need are Sessions, Units Ordered, and Unit Session Percentage — Amazon calculates the percentage for you, so you rarely need to do the division yourself except to sanity-check a discrepancy.

  • Sessions — visits to the detail page, counted once per visitor per day regardless of how many times they refreshed or returned.
  • Page Views — every page load, which can run higher than sessions if a shopper checks the listing more than once before buying.
  • Units Ordered — the count Amazon uses as the numerator. Not orders. Units.

Pull the report at the ASIN level if you sell one core product, and at the Parent level if you're comparing conversion across colors or sizes — the parent number is the one that matches what shoppers actually experienced across all the variation clicks combined.

A Worked Example

Say a listing had 8,200 sessions and 410 orders last month, and 40 of those orders were for two units each. Total units ordered comes to 450.

  • Order Session Percentage: 410 ÷ 8,200 × 100 = 5.0%
  • Unit Session Percentage: 450 ÷ 8,200 × 100 = 5.5%

Neither number is wrong. They're answering slightly different questions — how often a session turned into an order, versus how many units each session generated. If you're benchmarking against a competitor's public estimate, or against last quarter's number, use the same one both times. Mixing them is the single most common way sellers convince themselves their rate moved when it didn't.

Ad-Level Conversion Rate Is a Different Calculation

Everything above is the organic listing formula. Sponsored Products, Sponsored Display, and DSP reports calculate conversion using clicks or impressions as the denominator, not sessions, and attributed purchases as the numerator, not units ordered. Same shape of formula, different inputs — which is why a listing's Seller Central conversion rate almost never matches the conversion rate on an ad report for the same product, and shouldn't.

We see this daily running Amazon DSP as a managed service. Across 30 advertisers in our book in July 2026, the media delivered 6.04x return on ad spend — measured across the whole portfolio, not the best-performing line item — against 78.4 million impressions and a blended $1.42 CPC. The conversion side of that math produced 57,137 attributed purchases at a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition. None of those figures reconcile cleanly against a Seller Central Unit Session Percentage, because the denominator is clicks and impressions, not sessions, and the attribution window and dedup logic are different again once you look inside Amazon Marketing Cloud.

If you're trying to judge whether ads are helping your organic conversion rate, that's a fair question, but this formula can't answer it alone. It needs a holdout or a matched control, not a side-by-side of two percentages that were never measuring the same audience in the first place.

Mistakes That Skew the Number

  • Comparing mismatched date ranges. Sessions and units ordered have to come from the exact same window. A common error is pulling last-30-days sessions against this-month's orders.
  • Grouping by parent when you meant child, or child when you meant parent. Variation listings can show wildly different conversion rates ASIN to ASIN even though shoppers experienced them as one product.
  • Treating a third-party estimate as Seller Central truth. External rank trackers infer sessions from search-volume models. Seller Central counts real visits. They will not match, and the gap isn't a bug.
  • Reading ad-attributed conversions against organic sessions. Covered above — different formula, different denominator, different meaning.

We've made a version of this mistake ourselves: early in a DSP holdout test, reading a dip in last-click attributed conversions as proof the campaign underperformed, before the attribution window had even closed. Last-click can't tell you that on its own — it just tells you which purchases happened to get tagged before the count was pulled. That's part of why we reconcile in Amazon Marketing Cloud instead of trusting the attribution report at face value.

When the Number Looks Wrong

A conversion rate of 0% with real session traffic almost always means something structural broke, not that shoppers changed their minds en masse. Check, in order: is the buy box suppressed, is the listing out of stock or showing a shipping delay, did the main image or price get flagged, and did a variation get orphaned from its parent. Fix the structural issue before touching copy or images — a page can't convert what it isn't allowed to sell.

A sudden drop with no listing changes is usually a traffic-mix problem, not a conversion problem: a spike in low-intent sessions from a broad-match keyword, a lightning deal that pulled in bargain browsers, or a competitor's price drop pulling in comparison shoppers who bounce. Split sessions by source before you touch the listing — you may not have a conversion problem at all.

An unusually high number deserves a second look too. A tiny sessions count makes the percentage swing wildly on a handful of orders, and a short reporting outage can undercount sessions while orders still post normally, inflating the rate for a day or two until the report catches up.

Where Paid Media Fits Once You Trust the Number

Organic conversion rate tells you how well the listing does its job once someone lands on it. It doesn't tell you whether the traffic that got them there was worth paying for, or whether a display campaign moved anyone who wouldn't have bought anyway. Which one you should actually pick

Sellers running one listing on organic traffic only need the Business Reports export and the formula above — no tool required. Larger catalogs get more from Brand Analytics category comparisons. Brands running paid media, DSP especially, need a second layer on top: incrementality testing, because a click- or impression-based conversion rate can't tell you what the ad actually caused.

Common questions

What is the Amazon conversion rate formula?

Units ordered divided by sessions, multiplied by 100. Amazon's own report labels the result Unit Session Percentage rather than conversion rate, and it sits next to a separate Order Session Percentage that counts orders instead of units — the two only match when every order is for a single unit.

How do I calculate conversion rate on Amazon manually if I don't trust the report?

Pull Sessions and Units Ordered from Business Reports → Detail Page Sales and Traffic for the same date range, divide units by sessions, multiply by 100. The report does this automatically, so a manual calculation is mainly useful for spotting a stale cache or a date-range mismatch.

What's a good conversion rate on Amazon?

There's no single reliable figure — published benchmarks for Amazon range from roughly 2% to over 10% depending on category, price point, and whether the source is counting units or orders. Track your own listing against its own history and against Brand Analytics category data rather than a fixed target pulled from a blog post.

Why doesn't my advertising conversion rate match my Seller Central conversion rate?

They're different formulas. Seller Central divides units ordered by sessions. Ad reports divide attributed purchases by clicks or impressions, over a defined attribution window. Same math shape, different inputs — expect them to disagree, and don't average them.

Does conversion rate affect Amazon search ranking?

It's one signal among several, not the whole algorithm. Sales velocity, relevance, and availability all factor in alongside conversion performance, so improving conversion rate tends to help ranking indirectly rather than moving it on its own overnight.
