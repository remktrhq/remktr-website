What Is Amazon Sales Rank?

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1541 words · Written against what currently ranked for “what is an amazon sales rank”
The short answer

Amazon sales rank — also called Best Sellers Rank or BSR — is a number on a product page showing how that item sells against others in its category. Lower is better. It's driven almost entirely by recent sales velocity, not reviews, price, or ad clicks directly.

What the number actually measures

Amazon sales rank ranks a product against every other active listing in the same category, based on units sold. A rank of #1 means the best seller in that category right now. A rank of #40,000 means there are 39,999 items outselling it in that category. Lower is better — that trips people up because in most contexts a bigger number feels like more success.

The rank isn't fixed to a product. It's fixed to a category, at a moment in time. Amazon recalculates it frequently based on a mix of recent and historical sales, with recent sales weighted more heavily. A product can carry more than one rank if it sits in more than one category — a tumbler might show #1 in Tumblers & Water Glasses and #40 in the broader Kitchen & Dining category. Neither number is the real one. They're both real, for different slices of the catalog.

What it does not measure: page views, click-through rate, star rating, or review count. A product with excellent reviews and a mediocre sales rate will still sit behind a product with average reviews and strong recent sales. Rank tracks what sold, not what people thought of it.

A worked example

Say two products sit in the same subcategory. Product A sold 40 units in the last 24 hours and 900 over the past few months. Product B sold 15 units yesterday but 3,000 over the same stretch. Because recent velocity counts more than lifetime totals, Product A can outrank Product B today — even though B has sold more units overall. That's the part most explanations skip: rank answers "what's selling right now," not "what has sold the most, ever."

Depth matters too. A rank of #500 means something different depending on how big the category is. In a subcategory with 5,000 active listings, #500 puts you in the top 10%. In a subcategory with 50,000 listings, that same #500 puts you in the top 1%. Always check the category size before deciding whether a number is good or bad — the raw digit alone tells you almost nothing.

How to find it, and what counts as good

On the product detail page, scroll to the "Product information" or "Additional information" section. If the product ranks well enough, you'll see one or more lines like "#312 in Home & Kitchen." There's no separate dashboard for shoppers to look this up — it lives on the page itself, and it's only shown for products with enough sales history to generate a rank.

There's no universal "good" number, because it depends entirely on category size and how fast that category turns over. A rank of #2,000 in a niche subcategory can be excellent. A rank of #2,000 in Beauty or Home & Kitchen, where hundreds of thousands of SKUs compete, is mediocre. The honest way to judge it: track the trend for your own product over weeks, and compare your rank to the category leader and to your direct competitors in the same subcategory, not to some number you saw in a blog post.

Rank also isn't the same thing as search ranking. Search ranking decides what shows up when someone types a query. BSR decides what shows up on the Best Sellers list. They move in the same direction most of the time — because both track sales — but a product can have a strong BSR and a weak organic search position, or the reverse, at the same time.

What moves it and what doesn't

  • Recent sales velocity — moves it the most, and fastest
  • Lifetime sales — moves it, but with less weight than recent sales
  • Stockouts — a product that goes out of stock loses its sales rank quickly, even a short one
  • Price — no direct effect, only indirect through whether it drives more sales
  • Reviews and star rating — no direct effect, same caveat
  • Ad clicks and impressions — no direct effect; only the resulting purchase moves rank
  • Category size and depth — doesn't move your rank, but decides what a given number means

That last row of the pattern explains why ads and sales rank get confused so often. Running ads doesn't touch BSR directly. A purchase does. So a campaign only moves rank if it actually converts new sales, not just clicks. Across 30 advertisers we manage, the book ran a blended cost per acquisition of $5.49 on 57,137 attributed purchases in a single month — each one of those purchases is what nudges rank, not the media spend that produced it. That distinction matters if you're trying to explain a rank change to someone upstream: the spend is not the mechanism, the sale is.

When the number is bad news, or won't move

If rank suddenly gets worse (a bigger number), check for a stockout first — it's the single most common cause and the easiest to miss because the listing still looks live. Check next whether Amazon reclassified the product into a bigger, more competitive category; that alone can push the same sales volume into a worse-looking rank. If the product shows no rank at all, it usually means too little sales history yet, not that something is broken.

If rank isn't moving despite a real push — more traffic, a price test, new creative — resist the urge to credit or blame the rank shift for a change you made elsewhere. The mistake we've made ourselves: watching a BSR jump line up with a campaign launch during a holiday week and assuming the campaign caused it, when seasonal demand was moving at the same time. Rank alone can't separate the two. That's a correlation problem, not a rank problem, and it's exactly why last-click attribution and a single trend line aren't enough to prove a channel worked — you need a holdout or a matched control to know if the sale was incremental or would have happened anyway.

Which one you should actually pick

BSR is the right tool for sizing up a category or spotting your own sales trend — it's the wrong tool for proving what caused a sale. Sellers watching their own catalog should read it against category size, not in isolation. Anyone trying to prove an ad channel moved sales, not just rank, eventually needs incrementality testing rather than a trend line — which is the piece reMKTR builds around inside Amazon Marketing Cloud, for the subset of advertisers where that question is worth answering properly.

Common questions

What is a good Amazon sales rank?

There's no fixed threshold — it depends on the category. A rank in the low thousands can be excellent in a small subcategory and unremarkable in a massive one like Home & Kitchen. Judge it against your direct competitors in the same subcategory and against your own trend over time, not against a number you saw quoted elsewhere.

How do I find my Amazon sales rank?

Open the product detail page and look in the Product Information section. If the product has enough sales history, you'll see one or more category rankings listed there, such as "#312 in Home & Kitchen." There's no separate tool for shoppers — it's shown directly on the listing.

How often does Amazon sales rank update?

Amazon updates it frequently — sellers commonly observe changes within an hour or so of a sale — but Amazon has not published an exact interval, and it can vary by category. Treat any specific number you see quoted for update frequency with caution unless it comes from Amazon directly.

Does advertising improve sales rank?

Only indirectly, through the sales it generates. Impressions and clicks don't move BSR on their own — a completed purchase does. That's why a campaign's effect on rank depends entirely on whether it actually converts, and why it's worth separating clicks from purchases when judging whether the spend did anything.

Can you buy or manipulate Amazon sales rank?

No — attempting to manufacture sales or reviews to move rank violates Amazon's terms and risks the listing itself. The only durable way to move it is to sell more units to real buyers, which is a listing, pricing, and demand problem before it's anything else.
