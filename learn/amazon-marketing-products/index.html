Amazon Marketing Products: What They Are and How They Actually Fit Together

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1700 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon marketing products”
The short answer

Amazon's marketing products split into three groups: self-serve cost-per-click ads (Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display), programmatic display and video bought through Amazon DSP, and free owned assets like Brand Stores. Each has a different pricing model, minimum spend, and job in the funnel — they're not interchangeable.

The full list, and what each one is actually for

Amazon groups its marketing products into two buying mechanisms. Sponsored ads (Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display) are self-serve, cost-per-click, and live inside the Amazon advertising console. Amazon DSP buys display, online video, and streaming TV programmatically, priced on CPM, and is either self-managed with a DSP seat or run through a managed-service partner. Brand Stores sit outside both — they're free, owned pages that hold your catalog and brand story, and they don't buy any media themselves.

The mistake most people make reading Amazon's own product pages is treating this as a menu where you pick one. It's a stack. Sponsored Products catches demand at the point of search. Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display extend that into consideration and retargeting. DSP and streaming TV ads build the awareness that creates the search in the first place, and reach shoppers off Amazon too — on other apps and sites, not just on-site. Brand Stores are where all of that traffic lands once it's high-intent.

  • Sponsored Products — cost-per-click, no minimum spend, self-serve. The starting point for almost every seller.
  • Sponsored Brands — cost-per-click, requires Brand Registry, adds a headline and multi-product creative.
  • Sponsored Display — cost-per-click, retargets shoppers who viewed your listing or a competitor's.
  • Amazon DSP — CPM-based, programmatic, full-funnel, typically requires a seat or a managed-service partner.
  • Streaming TV ads — CPM-based, bought through DSP, top-of-funnel, runs on Prime Video and other streaming inventory.
  • Brand Store — free, owned, no media spend attached.
  • Amazon Marketing Cloud — not a media product at all. A clean-room measurement layer that lets you see whether sponsored ads and DSP are reaching the same people twice.

A worked example: what the stack looks like on a real account

Definitions only get you so far. Here's what the numbers look like when sponsored ads and DSP are running together on an actual book of business. Across 30 advertisers reMKTR manages, July 2026 spend delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, measured across the whole portfolio — not the single best-performing line item, which is the number most case studies quietly lead with instead.

That spend bought 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, with a blended $1.42 cost-per-click across the book. If you've seen a $0.41 CPC quoted for this category elsewhere, that figure is online-video only — it isn't what a full sponsored-ads-plus-DSP stack costs blended together, and quoting it without the rest of the mix is how budgets get set wrong before a campaign even launches.

On the conversion side, that same activity produced 57,137 attributed purchases at a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition, and 20.1% of those purchases came from a shopper new to the brand. That last number is the one worth sitting with: it's the difference between DSP that's finding new customers and DSP that's just retargeting people who were going to buy anyway. If your new-to-brand share is near zero, the media plan is working against itself, not for it.

What these products cost, and the minimums nobody puts on the homepage

Sponsored ads have no minimum spend — Amazon's own pricing page states you can start with as little as $10, and you're only charged when someone clicks. That's genuinely true and worth taking at face value for a first campaign.

DSP and managed-service advertising work differently, and this is where the published numbers thin out. Amazon's own guide states that advertising through a managed-service option with an Amazon account executive typically requires a minimum investment of $50,000 — that's Amazon's figure, from Amazon's own page, and it's the clearest minimum-spend number publicly stated in this category. What that page doesn't tell you is the ongoing fee structure once you're past that minimum: some partners charge flat platform fees, some charge a percentage of spend, some do both. A percentage-of-spend fee isn't a red flag by itself — it's how most media-buying services, including ours, are priced. The problem is when only the flat fee gets published and the percentage stays a quote-only conversation. At $100,000 a month in spend, one percentage point is $12,000 a year, and that's the number you should ask for in writing before you ask about anything else.

The mistake that's easy to make, because Amazon's own dashboards invite it

The most common error with Amazon marketing products isn't picking the wrong one — it's reading the results wrong. Last-click attribution, which is what the standard advertising console shows you, credits whichever ad a shopper clicked last before buying. It cannot tell you whether that purchase would have happened anyway. A shopper who saw a streaming TV ad, then searched the brand name, then clicked a Sponsored Products ad — last-click gives all the credit to the search ad and none to the video that actually created the demand.

This isn't a hypothetical mistake. It's the default setting on every Amazon Ads account, and plenty of experienced teams — including ours, early on — have reported DSP results using the same last-click logic as sponsored ads, which either overstates display's contribution or, more often, makes it look like it did nothing at all. The fix isn't a better dashboard. It's a different measurement method entirely: holdout tests and matched-market controls, run inside Amazon Marketing Cloud, comparing exposed audiences against unexposed ones. That's the only way to separate sales DSP actually created from sales it just happened to be standing near.

When the numbers look wrong, check these before you cut budget

If ROAS drops, check whether Sponsored Products and DSP are being reported separately and then added together — that double-counts any purchase where both touched the same customer, and it's the single most common reason a media plan looks worse in reconciliation than it did in two separate dashboards.

If cost-per-click looks fine but sales aren't following, check the funnel stage of what you're running. Sponsored Display and DSP retargeting will always look efficient because they're chasing warm traffic — that's not proof the campaign is growing the business, just that it's finding people close to the register.

If new-to-brand share is low, the creative or the audience is probably too narrow — you're spending awareness-stage budget on people who already know you. And if a managed-service quote won't state its percentage fee in writing, that's not a small detail to negotiate later. Get it before the contract, not after the first invoice.

Which one you should actually pick

Self-serve sponsored ads suit any seller starting out — no minimum, fast to launch, and Amazon's own tools are enough. DSP and full-funnel media suit brands that have already plateaued on sponsored ads and need proof, not assumption, that display is adding new customers. reMKTR runs that second stage as a managed service, holding real DSP seats and reconciling results in Amazon Marketing Cloud — worth knowing about once last-click stops answering the question you're actually asking.

Common questions

What's the difference between Sponsored Products and Amazon DSP?

Sponsored Products is cost-per-click and shows up in search results and product pages — it catches demand that already exists. Amazon DSP is CPM-based and programmatic, running display, video, and streaming TV both on and off Amazon, and it's built to create demand, not just capture it. Most brands need both, at different points in the funnel.

Do I need an agency or managed-service partner to run Amazon DSP?

Not strictly — DSP seats can be self-managed. But DSP is harder to run well than sponsored ads: it needs audience strategy, creative for multiple formats, and measurement work in Amazon Marketing Cloud to know if it's working. Amazon's own guidance points to a $50,000 minimum for its managed-service option, which is a reasonable signal of the scale where a partner starts to make sense.

How much does Amazon advertising cost overall?

Sponsored ads have no minimum and are pay-per-click, so you can start with a small budget. DSP and managed-service advertising are priced differently — often a base fee plus a percentage of spend, or a seat fee — and the percentage is rarely posted publicly. Ask for the full fee structure, not just the headline number, before committing spend.

How do I know if DSP is actually adding sales, not just recapturing my own sponsored ads clicks?

Check new-to-brand purchase rate and run a holdout test — hold a matched group of shoppers back from DSP exposure and compare their purchase rate to the exposed group. Last-click reporting in the standard console can't answer this question; it wasn't built to. Amazon Marketing Cloud, used with holdouts or matched controls, can.

What's the most common mistake brands make with Amazon marketing products?

Running DSP and sponsored ads as if they're separate line items and reading last-click credit as proof of what each one did. It's an easy mistake because it's the default view in the dashboard. The fix is reconciling both in Amazon Marketing Cloud so a shopper touched by both channels only gets counted once.
