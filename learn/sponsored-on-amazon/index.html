The short answer

On Amazon, "sponsored" means a paid ad you bid for by click or impression, not a fixed placement. There are three types: Sponsored Products for single listings in search, Sponsored Brands for a multi-product banner with your logo, and Sponsored Display for retargeting on and off Amazon.

What "sponsored" actually means on Amazon

Every "Sponsored" label on Amazon marks an ad someone won in an auction. It's not a paid placement guarantee and it's not free promotion — it's a bid, matched against other advertisers targeting the same keyword, product, or audience, in real time. There's no flat monthly or setup fee for Sponsored Products or Sponsored Brands: you set a maximum bid, and you're charged only when someone clicks.

Amazon runs three sponsored formats inside its self-service ads console: Sponsored Products (a single listing shown in search results and on product pages), Sponsored Brands (a banner with your logo and headline across several products, top of search), and Sponsored Display (native or video ads that follow a shopper on and off Amazon based on browsing behavior). All three sit apart from Amazon DSP, which is a separate programmatic buying system, not part of the self-service console.

Eligibility differs by format. Sponsored Products is open to professional sellers, vendors, book vendors, KDP authors, and agencies, with no brand registration required. Sponsored Brands and most Sponsored Display creative formats require Brand Registry. None of the three will show your ad if the product is out of stock or isn't eligible for the featured offer — sponsored budget spent on an out-of-stock ASIN simply doesn't spend.

Sponsored Products vs. Sponsored Brands vs. Sponsored Display

The three formats solve different problems, and mixing them up is the most common early mistake. Sponsored Products is the workhorse for direct sales on a specific listing. Sponsored Brands is for building headline presence across a catalog. Sponsored Display is for reaching shoppers who've already looked at your product, or a competitor's, whether they're still on Amazon or not.

The CPC you've seen quoted is probably measuring the wrong thing

If you've come across "$0.41 CPC" as a benchmark for Amazon sponsored advertising, be careful with it. That number typically comes from online video placements alone — one narrow slice of inventory, not a full account. Across a full book of Sponsored Display and Amazon DSP campaigns, reMKTR's July 2026 numbers looked very different: 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, blending down to a $1.42 CPC once every placement type is included, not just the cheapest one.

The same book returned a 6.04x ROAS across 30 advertisers — measured across the entire portfolio, not pulled from a best-performing line item — with a blended cost per acquisition of $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them new to the brand. That's a DSP-inclusive number, worth saying plainly: Sponsored Display shares audience mechanics with DSP but is a different product with a different console. The point isn't that this number is yours too. It's that a single quoted CPC or CPA from a listicle rarely survives contact with a real, mixed account.

The mistake almost everyone makes, including us

The most common misreading of sponsored performance is treating a last-click "attributed sale" as proof the ad caused the purchase. It doesn't prove that. A shopper who saw a Sponsored Display ad and would have bought the product anyway still shows up as a win in last-click reporting — the report has no way to know the difference.

We've made this mistake ourselves. It's exactly why reMKTR now reconciles DSP and sponsored ad exposure inside Amazon Marketing Cloud: when a shopper sees both a display impression and a sponsored ad before buying, last-click reporting can credit both, inflating the apparent return. AMC lets you see the overlap instead of double-counting it. Holdout tests and matched controls can tell you whether the spend added incremental sales; last-click attribution, on its own, cannot and never could.

A second common mistake, more mechanical: ignoring the listing itself. Amazon's own guidance is specific — products need at least five reviews at 3.5 stars or higher, four or more zoomable images, and a title under roughly 60 characters to perform well in Sponsored Products. Amazon has also published that suggested products, when advertised, are 321 times more likely to generate an attributed sale than products not suggested by its own systems. Spend follows the listing; a strong ad on a weak listing rarely recovers the difference.

When the numbers look wrong, here's what to check first

  • Zero or near-zero impressions: check inventory and buy box eligibility before touching bids — an out-of-stock or ineligible ASIN won't show regardless of budget.
  • High spend, few clicks: your bid is probably below the suggested range for that keyword or product target; check the console's current bid guidance rather than a number from an old guide.
  • Clicks but no sales: the listing is usually the problem, not the ad — check review count, image quality, and price competitiveness against the featured offer.
  • Great ROAS, low new-to-brand share: the ad may be capturing demand that would have converted organically anyway; this is where incrementality testing, not more spend, is the right next step.
  • A quoted CPC or CPA that doesn't match what you're seeing: check what inventory subset it was measured on. A single-placement figure and a blended-book figure are not the same number, and most published benchmarks don't say which one they are.

Where Amazon DSP fits once sponsored ads aren't the whole picture

Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display are all self-service, auction-priced by click or impression, and run inside the Amazon Ads console. Amazon DSP is a different system entirely — programmatic display and video, bought through actual DSP seats, usually layered alongside sponsored ads rather than replacing them. reMKTR runs Amazon DSP as a managed service, holding 109 live advertiser seats, and reconciles DSP spend against sponsored spend inside Amazon Marketing Cloud so the two stop double-counting the same shopper. reMKTR is part of the Full Circle group, which has managed more than $500M in Amazon ad spend across 100+ brands, 70+ of them live right now. None of this is relevant if you're only running Sponsored Products or Sponsored Brands today — it starts to matter the moment DSP enters the media mix and you need to know, honestly, whether it added anything.

Which one you should actually pick

Sponsored Products suits anyone with a single listing and a budget — no design work, no brand registration. Sponsored Brands suits a registered brand that wants headline space across several SKUs. Sponsored Display suits retargeting shoppers who browsed but didn't buy. None of the three, on their own, prove incrementality — that needs holdout testing, which sits closer to DSP than to sponsored ads.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What's the difference between Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands?

Sponsored Products promotes one listing at a time and appears in search results and on product pages — no Brand Registry needed. Sponsored Brands requires Brand Registry and shows a banner with your logo and several products together, usually at the top of search.

Is Sponsored Display the same as Amazon DSP?

No. Sponsored Display is self-service, run from the same console as Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands, with a set menu of audiences and creative. Amazon DSP is programmatic buying through actual DSP seats, with far more granular audience and inventory control, and it's usually run either in-house by a dedicated team or through a managed service.

How much do sponsored ads cost on Amazon?

Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands have no flat monthly or setup fee — you set a maximum bid and pay only on click, so your real cost depends on the auction for your specific keywords or products. Sponsored Display prices by click or impression depending on the goal selected in the console. Check the current suggested bid range for your category rather than any single number quoted elsewhere; it moves by category, competition, and time of year.

Do I need Brand Registry to run sponsored ads?

Not for Sponsored Products. You do need Brand Registry for Sponsored Brands and for most Sponsored Display creative formats.

Why did my Sponsored Products ad get zero impressions?

The most common reason is inventory or buy box eligibility — ads only show when the product is in stock and eligible to be the featured offer. The second most common reason is a bid set well below the current suggested range for that keyword or product target.
